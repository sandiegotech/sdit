# Arc — CS 110 Programming Fundamentals

## The course in one paragraph

At the start you can describe what you want a machine to do. At the end you can say it so precisely that the machine does it, and you can prove it did. The catalog register is an introduction to programming — variables, control flow, functions, data, debugging — and every one of those arrives in this order, by hand, on real problems. The teaching frame is Speaking to Machines: the AI assistant is open on the table from the first hour, instrumented, and critiqued, and the skill this course grades is direction and verification. You leave able to tell a machine (or a model) exactly what you want, read every line of what comes back, test it, and explain it unaided.

## The made thing

A small working program that does something you actually wanted done. Not an exercise: a thing you will keep running after the course ends. Examples the days offer: a script that reads your bank export and tells you what you spent on food; a tide-and-swell summary for your spot pulled from a public buoy feed; a tool that renames and sorts a folder of photos by date; a flashcard drill for a Canon text. "Finished" means three things ship together: the program, which runs on someone else's machine from your instructions; a one-page write-up of what it does and how you know it works; and your **verification log** — the record, kept from Day 2, of what you asked the assistant for, what came back, what was wrong, and how you found out. The program is demonstrated to a classmate or friend who runs it without your help, and defended: any line, explained on request.

Day 2 starts the log. Day 5 has a first small program running. Day 11 scopes the made thing with a written brief. Day 10 has its first version reading real data. Day 12 tests it. Day 15 ships it. By Day 5 you know the shape of the thing you are going to make; by Day 10 it runs.

## The unassisted examination

Twice, with the tools closed. **Day 8:** read a fifteen-line program on paper and write down exactly what it prints; then write a small function by hand (a loop, a branch, a return). **Day 14:** find and fix the bug in a program given to you, and write a function from a one-sentence specification, both on paper or in an editor with no assistant. These two are examined because they are the two halves of the course's claim: that you can read what the machine was told, and that you can tell it yourself. A learner who can only do either with the assistant has learned to operate a tool, not to program. The exams are short, graded as pass or revise, and repeated at the annual gate.

## The build sequence

**Day 5:** a twenty-line Python program you wrote with the assistant open, that loops over something of yours (a list of tasks, a text file, a folder) and makes a decision about each item. Every line read aloud and explained. The verification log has five entries.

**Day 10:** the first version of your made thing. It reads real data you have (a CSV, a text export, a folder) into lists and dictionaries, is divided into named functions, and prints something true that you did not know before you ran it. The assistant wrote some of it; you can point to which lines, and to the two places where it was wrong.

**Day 15:** the made thing shipped — program, write-up, verification log — with tests that pass, run by someone else from your instructions, and defended line by line.

**How the AI tools are used:** open from Day 2, always with the log beside them. The pattern every day is the same: say what you want (direction), run what comes back, read every line, test it against a case you chose, and record where it diverged from what you meant (verification). Days 7, 11, 12, and 13 are explicitly about the assistant's failure modes: confident wrong fixes, code that answers a different question than the one you asked, tests that test nothing, and plausible output that is not correct. **How the human is examined unaided:** Days 8 and 14, tools closed, as above — plus the standing rule that any line of your program may be explained on request at any time.

## Open questions for Brandon

1. **Language.** The arc assumes Python. Is that the Institute's language for CS 110 and CS 210, or should the days be written so the learner can use any language the assistant speaks? The unassisted exams are far easier to standardize in one language.
2. **The default made thing.** The arc offers four examples and lets the learner choose. Should the course instead have one shared default (the buoy-and-tide summary from CDIP data is the most San Diego of them) so classmates can run each other's programs on Day 15?
3. **Running Python without installing.** Day 1 promised "you will not install anything today." Day 2 needs Python running. Should the Institute host or point to a specific browser-based runner, or is "install Python, here is how" acceptable on Day 2?
