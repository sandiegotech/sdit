# Arc — MATH 220 Probability & Statistics

## The course in one paragraph

At the start, the learner says "probably" and "about" and has never been scored on either. At the end, the learner can count the ways a thing can happen, update a belief as evidence arrives, price a bet, say what "three feet at fifteen seconds" is a statistic of, read a bell curve and know where it came from, put an honest error bar on a measurement, tell a real effect from noise in an A/B test, fit a line through buoy data and know what it cannot say, and make a public forecast in probabilities that is scored against what happened. That is the catalog register, probability theory, distributions, inference, regression. The teaching frame is forecasting and games — swell models, prediction markets, poker, A/B tests — the native mathematics of the AI age, in which every model's output is a probability and the only honest test of a forecaster is the score.

## The made thing

A forecast with skin in the game: made, tracked, and scored in public. From Day 6 on, each learner posts weekly probabilistic forecasts on a shared public page — the surf height at a named spot on a named day, the marine layer's clearing hour, an election or a market, a product metric from a running A/B test — each with a stated probability or a range with stated confidence, each resolved by a source named in advance. The forecasts are scored with Brier and log scores as they resolve; by Day 14 every learner has a calibration curve. "Finished" means at least eight resolved forecasts, the score, the calibration curve, a one-page account of the model behind the forecasts (base rates, the regression, the updates made and why), and a short public write-up of the worst miss. It is defended on Day 15 before the room and the ledger, and the ledger stays public. It enters the portfolio.

Day 1 introduces it (you will forecast and be scored). Day 4 scopes it (choose your domain and your resolution source; state your base rates). Day 6 starts it (the first forecasts post). Day 13 adds the regression and the ensemble. Day 14 scores it. Day 15 is the defense.

## The unassisted examination

Two sittings, tools closed. After Day 10: counting and sample spaces, a conditional probability and an independence check, a Bayes update on a real case, an expectation and a variance, a binomial and a normal probability read from a table, a confidence interval formed and interpreted. After Day 14: a hypothesis test read and criticized, a regression line's slope and residual interpreted with what it cannot claim, a Brier score computed and a calibration curve read. These are examined unaided because a probability has to be reasoned, not retrieved; a model will produce a number for any question, and the learner must be able to say whether it is the right question and whether the number is calibrated.
