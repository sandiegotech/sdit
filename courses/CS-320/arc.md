# Arc — CS 320 Human-AI Collaboration

## The course in one paragraph

By the seventh semester you have used the assistant in every course for three years. At the start of this one, that use is a habit. At the end it is a practice: you can say, for any piece of work, what you delegated and why, how you verified it, where your taste overruled the model's default, and what it cost — and you can defend each of those calls out loud. The catalog register is an advanced practicum in human-AI collaboration: delegation, verification, evaluation, taste. The teaching frame is Working with Intelligence: the studied practice of working with machine intelligence, the discipline the whole age runs on. The model for the practice is older than the machine — the Renaissance bottega, where a master ran a room of apprentices, wrote the brief, inspected the work, and signed it. You now run a bottega of one, with an apprentice that never sleeps and never says no, and this course makes you the master of it.

## The made thing

A substantial work produced with AI as instrument — with a full account of what was delegated, what was verified, and why the judgment calls were yours. The work is in your concentration and is yours to choose on Day 3: a component of your CAP 401 capstone, or a standalone piece — a short film with its edit and score, a working tool with users, a designed object through to fabrication, a long essay for HUM 401 — large enough that you could not finish it alone in twelve weeks and small enough that you could finish it with help. The account is the **ledger**, opened Day 2 and kept every working day: for each delegation, what you asked, what came back, how you checked it, what you changed, and what it cost you in time and in skill you did not build. "Finished" means the work exists and is shown, and the ledger is complete enough that an examiner can pick any passage, shot, function, or joint in the work and find the entry that says who made it and how it was verified. It is exhibited in your concentration's form and defended in a thirty-minute oral examination against the ledger.

Day 1 names the practice. Day 2 opens the ledger. Day 3 chooses the work. Day 5 has the brief written and the first delegation verified. Day 10 has half the work made and one piece remade by hand. Day 15 shows the work and defends the account. The learner knows what they are making by Day 3 and has begun by Day 4.

## The unassisted examination

Twice, tools closed. **Day 8:** produce one unit of your own work unaided — a page of the essay, a scene's shot list, a function, a part drawing — in ninety minutes, and compare it to the delegated version of the same unit. The comparison is written up and goes in the ledger. **Day 14:** an oral examination: the examiner picks three entries from your ledger and you justify each delegation, reproduce the verification, and name the judgment call, with no tool open. These are examined because the course's claim is that you can only judge work you could do, and only defend calls you actually made. A learner whose ledger says "verified" but who cannot reproduce the check has delegated the judgment too. Pass or revise; the Day 14 examination is also the model for the capstone defense.

## The build sequence

**Day 5:** the ledger is running with at least twenty entries. The work has a written brief — intent, constraints, what counts as done, what counts as wrong — in the form of Nelson's memorandum: enough that an apprentice could act without asking. The first delegated chunk is made, and verified by a method you can name (test, recomputation, source check, a second model, your own eye) and recorded.

**Day 10:** half the work exists. An evaluation set — ten to thirty cases you wrote, with the answers you would accept — has been run against the delegated output at least twice. One piece of the work has been remade by hand because the delegated version was average, and the ledger says what "average" meant.

**Day 15:** the work, shown in its form; the ledger, complete; the account — three pages on what was delegated, what was verified, and why the calls were yours — defended for thirty minutes.

**How the AI tools are used:** everywhere, as the subject. The course does not assume a particular product; you use whichever assistant you have, and the ledger records which. The practice drilled each week: brief before delegation (Day 4), verification at the seams (Day 5), adversarial reading for agreement (Day 6), evaluation sets (Day 7), taste against the default (Days 9, 10), cost accounting (Day 11), provenance (Day 12), and abstention (Day 13). **How the human is examined unaided:** Day 8, a unit of the work made by hand; Day 14, the oral defense; and throughout, the standing rule that any entry marked "verified" may be re-verified in front of the examiner.
