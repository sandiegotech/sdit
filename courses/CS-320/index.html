CS 320 — Human-AI Collaboration

Computation & Intelligent Systems · 4 units · Semester 7 · Required core — everyone takes it

Overview

By Year 4 you have used the AI assistant in every course for three years. That use is a habit. This course makes it a practice — studied, drilled, and graded. Delegation: what to hand off, and how to write the brief so it comes back right. Verification: how you know, at the seams where the delegated piece meets your own. Evaluation: writing down what good looks like before you look. Taste: choosing against the model’s average on purpose, and being able to say why. The model for all of it is older than the machine: the Renaissance workshop, where a master wrote the brief, assigned the parts, inspected the work, and signed it. You now run a workshop of one, with an apprentice that never sleeps and never says no.

The teaching frame is Working with Intelligence. The course names no product; you use whichever assistant you have, and from the second week you keep a ledger of every delegation — what you asked, what came back, how you checked it, what you changed, what it cost. The ledger is the spine of the made thing. Twice the tools close: once to make a unit of the work by hand and set it beside the delegated version, and once to defend three ledger entries out loud to an examiner. You can only judge work you could do, and only defend calls you actually made.

One day a week, about ten minutes a sitting, and it ends in a made thing: a substantial work in your concentration, made with AI as instrument, with a full account of what was yours.

The Two Registers

  • Catalog register: Advanced practicum in human-AI collaboration — delegation, verification, evaluation, taste.
  • Teaching frame: Working with Intelligence — the studied practice of working with machine intelligence, the discipline the whole age runs on.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course you can:

  1. Write a brief that states intent, constraints, and what done looks like, and delegate against it — to a model or a person — so the work comes back as meant. → Command of Intelligence · Written communication
  2. Verify delegated work by a method you can name and reproduce, and build an evaluation set that makes your standard explicit. → Command of Intelligence · Judgment · Critical thinking
  3. Produce a unit of the work unaided and judge the delegated version against it. → Workmanship
  4. Account for the cost of each delegation — in time, money, and skill not built — and decide which you would make again. → Judgment
  5. Keep a complete ledger of authorship and defend any entry in it, out loud, with no tool open. → Judgment · Workmanship · Oral communication

Each outcome maps to the five institutional outcomes (Judgment, Command of Intelligence, Workmanship, Articulation, Breadth of Mind) — the assessment spine of the degree and its accreditation evidence file.

The Made Thing

A substantial work produced with AI as instrument — a component of your capstone or a standalone piece in your concentration — with a full account of what was delegated, what was verified, and why the judgment calls were yours. The account is the ledger, kept every working day, complete enough that an examiner can pick any passage, shot, function, or joint in the work and find the entry that says who made it and how it was checked. The work is exhibited in your concentration’s form and defended in a thirty-minute oral examination against the ledger. It enters your portfolio and is reviewed at the annual gate.

How You’re Assessed

  • The ledger, kept weekly and read weekly — the practice made visible.
  • The work and its account, exhibited and defended for thirty minutes against the ledger.
  • Unassisted examinations — one unit of the work made by hand and compared, and an oral defense of three ledger entries with no tool open. The institution certifies the human, so part of this course happens with the tools closed.
  • Everything lands in your portfolio, reviewed against the five outcomes at the end-of-year gate and carried toward the capstone defense.
