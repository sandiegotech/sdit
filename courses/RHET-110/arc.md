# Arc — RHET 110 Writing Seminar

## The course in one paragraph

At the start you can write a paragraph that is more or less true. At the end you can write one that lands: a reader who did not want to agree with you finishes it, understands exactly what you claimed, can point to the evidence, and has to argue with the thing you actually said. The catalog register is college writing — argument, evidence, structure, revision. The teaching frame is heavy output, line-edited: you write every week, everything comes back marked, and you rewrite. The course has one working premise, that writing is thinking made auditable, and one method, revision, because clarity on the page and clarity in the head are trained the same way. Nothing here is about grammar for its own sake. It is about the distance between the first draft and the final, and about learning to see that distance yourself.

## The made thing

A portfolio of four revised essays, each presented as first draft and final side by side, with every line-edit that moved it from one to the other kept and visible. Finished means: four essays of 600 to 1,000 words, each in two states; the Week 1 baseline piece, unrevised, as the measure; the unassisted examination page; and a one-page preface in which you name, in plain sentences, what changed between your drafts and your finals and why. The portfolio is exhibited two ways. It goes to the annual gate as a document. And on Day 15 you read one revised passage aloud to the seminar, draft first, then final, and take questions about why each change was made. The reader is the exhibition.

Day 1 introduces the portfolio and sets the baseline piece. Day 4 scopes it: four essays, four kinds (a made thing described, an argument, a review, a letter to one reader), the first drafted that week. Day 6 returns the first essay marked and the revising begins; by Day 7 the first pair is complete. Day 14 assembles the portfolio and writes the preface. By Day 15 the learner is not beginning the artifact; they are defending it.

## The unassisted examination

Day 11, with the tools closed: forty-five minutes, one prompt given in the room, one handwritten or plain-text page, about 400 words. The prompt is a claim about something concrete in the learner's own semester — a Workshop build, a problem from Linear Algebra, a book from HUM 201 — to be argued, with evidence, in a shape the reader can follow. No notes, no model, no revision time. Why this and why here: by Week 11 the learner has been line-edited for ten weeks and has used a model as a reader for two. The institution certifies the human, so it needs one page written by the human alone, mid-course, before the last essays are drafted. The page also serves a second purpose: it is the voice sample against which the portfolio is checked. The final drafts must sound like the person who wrote the exam.

**How it is line-edited.** The editor does not rewrite. The editor marks, and the writer rewrites. Six marks, and only six, so that a marked page can be read at a glance and so that the writer learns to make the marks before the editor does:

- **CUT** — the words do no work.
- **VAGUE** — a reader cannot picture it or check it.
- **CLAIM?** — you asserted; where is the evidence?
- **SO WHAT** — true, and the reader does not know why it is here.
- **PLAINER** — a shorter, more common word was available.
- **LAND** — the sentence ends on its weakest word.

Plus one margin note per paragraph, in a full sentence, saying what the paragraph is trying to do and whether it did. Every piece is edited twice: once by a peer, using the same six marks, and once by the master. The two sets of marks come back together. Where they disagree, the writer decides, and says why in the next draft's note. Before any piece is submitted it is read aloud, by the writer, to one other person, and that is a rule, not a suggestion.

**The machine.** From Day 12 onward a model may be used as a reader: it may apply the six marks to a draft and explain them. It may not write a sentence that enters a draft. The portfolio holds the model's marks beside the writer's verdicts on them, so a reviewer can see the judgment exercised. The unassisted page from Day 11 is the control.

**The specimens (the forensic cases line-edited in class).** Each is a before-and-after that exists in the historical record, so the class can line-edit a master's draft against the master's own final: Lincoln's Nicolay draft of the Gettysburg Address against the Bliss text; Jefferson's rough draft of the Declaration with Franklin's and Adams's hands on it; Strunk's own examples in the 1918 *Elements*; Orwell's parody of Ecclesiastes against the King James original; Franklin's account of rewriting *Spectator* essays from notes and comparing the result. Contemporary pieces of writing, chosen by the learners, are brought to Day 8 and Day 13 as doors, not as on-page specimens.
