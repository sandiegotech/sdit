RHET 110 — Writing Seminar

Rhetoric & Epistemics · 4 units · Semester 3 · Required core — everyone takes it

Overview

Writing is thinking made auditable. A thought in your head can be vague and still feel finished; the same thought on a page cannot hide. Fifteen weeks of college writing — argument, evidence, structure, revision — taught the only way it has ever been learned: by writing a great deal and having every sentence read back to you marked. You write every week. Everything comes back within the week with every line touched. Then you rewrite.

The specimens are masters’ drafts beside their finals, because the record keeps them: Lincoln’s Gettysburg draft with its corrections, Jefferson’s rough draft with Franklin’s hand on it, Franklin teaching himself by rewriting the Spectator from notes. You line-edit them before you line-edit yourself, and you learn six marks that say almost everything an editor ever needs to say. From Week 12 a model may read your drafts and mark them; it may never write them. The distance between your first draft and your final has to be yours, and the course is built so that a reviewer can see it is.

One day a week, about ten minutes a sitting, plus the writing — which is the course. It ends the way every SDIT course ends: in a made thing, read aloud and defended.

The Two Registers

  • Catalog register: College writing — argument, evidence, structure, revision.
  • Teaching frame: Heavy output, line-edited. You write constantly; everything comes back marked.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course you can:

  1. State a claim in one sentence, support it with particulars a reader can check, and order the whole so a reader always knows where they are. → Articulation
  2. Cut, reorder, and rewrite your own draft so that a reader who did not want to agree finishes it and argues with what you actually said. → Articulation
  3. Judge the quality of evidence in a piece of writing — your own or a master’s — and say what is asserted, what is shown, and what is missing. → Judgment
  4. Use a model as a reader of your drafts, rule on each of its marks, and defend the rulings. → Judgment
  5. Write a clear, argued page unaided, in a fixed time, with the tools closed. → Articulation

Each outcome maps to the five institutional outcomes (Judgment, Command of Intelligence, Workmanship, Articulation, Breadth of Mind) — the assessment spine of the degree and its accreditation evidence file.

The Made Thing

A portfolio of four revised essays — a made thing described, an argument, a review, and a letter to one reader — each presented as first draft and final side by side, with every line-edit kept visible, so the distance is the exhibit. With it: your Week 1 baseline piece, unrevised, as the measure; your unassisted examination page; and a one-page preface naming what changed and why. On the last day you read one passage aloud to the seminar, draft first, then final, and answer for every change. The portfolio enters your record and is reviewed at the annual gate.

How You’re Assessed

  • A piece every week, returned within the week with every sentence marked — six marks, one margin note per paragraph — then revised. The work is the writing and the rewriting; there is no quiz.
  • Four essays in two states each, graded on the final with the first draft beside it, so the revision is what is assessed.
  • The portfolio and the reading, defended aloud in seminar on the last day.
  • An unassisted examination in writing. AI tools are open as readers from Week 12; in Week 11 you write one argued page in forty-five minutes with the tools closed. The institution certifies the human, so part of your writing happens unaided, and that page is the voice sample your portfolio is checked against.
  • Everything lands in your portfolio, reviewed against the five outcomes at the end-of-year gate.
