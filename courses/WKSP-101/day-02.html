Day 02 — The Room Has a Master in It

Ten minutes on the page. One cup from a foundry, one honest fact about this course, one person to find.

Look

Turn the cup over in your mind. On one side, the finished thing, handed to the one who asked for it. On the other, the room it came out of: tools on the wall, a fire, and people who had learned the work from someone standing next to them. That is the Workshop, and it has been that for 2,500 years.

One idea

Here is the honest fact about this course. The Workshop is a room with tools and a master in it. The page you are reading is not the room.

Every other course this semester can mostly happen on the page: you read, you watch, you think, you answer. This one cannot. The catalog calls it a project-based fabrication and design practicum, and a practicum needs a bench, a tool you are not yet good with, and a person who is. The page carries the brief, the method, the critique, and the log. The making happens somewhere with sawdust in it.

So the first build of this course is finding the room. If you are taking this with the Institute, the room is ours and the master is in it. If you are taking it alone, you have the same assignment Hephaestus’s apprentices had: find the nearest room and the nearest master. A garage with a neighbor who welds. A community shop. A bike co-op, a sewing circle, a makerspace with a laser cutter and a volunteer who knows which end is hot. A parent. Then do the smallest real version of the thing in that room, with that person watching. Not a video of it.

The master matters more than the tools. A tool teaches you when you are wrong; a master teaches you what to try next. Day 3 will ask you to scope the semester’s build. Walk in there knowing whose bench it will sit on.

Carry the question

For the rest of today, carry this one:

Who is the nearest person who could show you a tool you don’t know how to use?

Answer in a line or a page, or not at all. What you write is kept in your Notebook.

That’s it. Day 2 is done.

If you’re still curious

Three doors. Open one, or none — they’ll be here tomorrow too.

Read the real thing. The Iliad, Book 18, Hephaestus at the forge, in the Lectio edition on the Lectio app or free in Samuel Butler’s translation. Thetis arrives with a brief and a deadline, and the god’s answer is the first shop conversation in literature.

Watch a workshop at scale. Mechanical Engineering at duPont (1950, Prelinger Archives). Twenty minutes of a company film, worth it for the shots of men teaching men at machines. The room got bigger; the arrangement did not change.

Ask someone. Ask a tradesperson: “What’s the first thing you’d teach an apprentice?” It is almost never a technique. Listen for what it is instead.
