CAP 402 — Thesis / Venture Capstone II

Field Term & Capstone · 2 units · Semester 8 · Required core — everyone takes it

Overview

The year-long work is finished and defended in public before faculty and outside examiners. A thesis, a system that runs, or a venture that exists — the form was chosen in CAP 401 and does not change now. Twenty minutes with the thing in the room, then an hour of questions from a panel that includes someone with a body of work in your field and no reason to be kind. Recorded, published, examined. The five outcomes, demonstrated at once. In HUM 402, the same semester, the work is argued against four years of reading; here it is argued on its own terms.

This is not a subject course. The fifteen days are fifteen weekly checkpoints on finishing: deciding what finished means and holding to it, naming the examiners, cutting, meeting the world, writing the account that stands without you, stating the claim unaided, writing your examiners’ hardest questions yourself, freezing the work, sending it ahead, rehearsing, defending, correcting, letting go. Each day carries one thing from someone who finished and was examined — Darwin publishing his “Abstract,” the examiners’ names on Curie’s title page, Wilbur Wright silencing Le Mans in two minutes, Faraday with the candle lit on the bench, five witnesses on the sand at Kitty Hawk — and one question to carry.

The course ends the way the degree ends: in a made thing, defended out loud, in public, and in a portfolio that is the actual credential.

The Two Registers

  • Catalog register: Capstone II — completion and public defense of the original work before faculty and outside examiners.
  • Teaching frame: The finished work, defended in public. A thesis, a system that runs, or a venture that exists — presented against the whole four-year sequence.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course you can:

  1. Bring an original work to a fixed, finished, defensible state by a date — deciding what finished means, cutting what will not be, and freezing the rest. → Workmanship · Judgment
  2. Write the account of a made thing — thesis, system paper, or venture memo — that lets a stranger judge or rebuild it with you gone. → Articulation · Written communication · Quantitative reasoning
  3. State the work’s strongest objections yourself, answer them in the account, and answer the ones you missed in writing, unaided. → Judgment · Critical thinking
  4. Defend the work in public before examiners, without notes, answering the question asked — and present it against the whole four-year sequence in HUM 402. → Articulation · Breadth of Mind · Oral communication
  5. Account for what machine intelligence did in the work and what you did without it, and show the difference under examination. → Command of Intelligence

Each outcome maps to the five institutional outcomes (Judgment, Command of Intelligence, Workmanship, Articulation, Breadth of Mind) — the assessment spine of the degree and its accreditation evidence file.

The Made Thing

The defended original work itself, in four parts: the work — a thesis at the length its question needs, a system running without you for a real user and measured doing it, or a venture legally formed with users or customers who are not your friends; the account — the thesis, or a paper or memo of 5,000 to 8,000 words that stands without you, frozen in Week 9 and delivered to the examiners two weeks before the defense; the defense — twenty minutes with the thing in the room and an hour of questions before a panel of at least three, including one outside examiner, held in public and recorded; and the examiners’ report with your corrections, signed off before graduation. A learner working alone online runs this as a self-set work with found examiners — two people with bodies of work who are not your friends, one inside the field and one well outside it, asked by Week 2, sent the account two weeks ahead, and given ninety minutes on a recorded call or in a borrowed room with the public invited. The recording and the account are published together. The standard is the same: people who could not be fooled looked at it and said what they saw.

How You’re Assessed

  • Fifteen weekly checkpoints, each with the week’s document in hand — the finished list, the panel, the cut list, the account, the claim, the five hardest questions, the freeze, the delivery.
  • The claim in 300 words, written unaided in Week 6 — one sitting, tools closed — and read beside the account’s abstract; the unaided one is the one examined.
  • The frozen work and account, versioned and archived in Week 9, delivered to the examiners in Week 10. Nothing is sent after.
  • The public defense in Week 13 — no notes beyond the slides, no device on the table, questions unseen. The institution certifies the human, so the human is observed alone.
  • The examiners’ report — pass, pass with corrections, or not yet — and your corrections, completed and signed off; unanswered questions answered in writing, unaided, within a week.
  • Everything lands in your portfolio, reviewed against the five outcomes at the final gate. The portfolio is the credential; the transcript is its interface.
