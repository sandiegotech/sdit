# Arc — CAP 402 Thesis / Venture Capstone II

## The course in one paragraph

At the start you have an accepted proposal, a first working piece, and fifteen weeks. At the end you have a finished original work — a thesis, a system that runs, or a venture that exists — defended in public before faculty and outside examiners, with the examiners' report and your corrections in the portfolio beside it. The catalog register is Capstone II: completion and public defense of the original work before faculty and outside examiners. The teaching frame is the finished work, defended in public, and presented against the whole four-year sequence. This is not a subject course. The fifteen days are fifteen weekly checkpoints on finishing: deciding what finished means and holding to it, writing the account that stands without you, anticipating the examiners, freezing the work, sending it ahead, rehearsing, defending, correcting, and letting go. Each day still carries one thing to experience from someone who finished and was examined, and one question. HUM 402 runs in the same semester and is where the work is argued against four years of reading; CAP 402 is where it is argued on its own terms.

## The made thing

**The defended original work itself** — the artifact from the catalog, and the terminal demonstration of all five outcomes. It has four parts, all required:

- **The work.** A thesis at the length the question needs; a system that runs without you in the room and has been measured doing a real job for a real user; a venture that is legally formed and has users or customers who are not your friends. Finished means the "finished" list from the proposal is checked off, or each unchecked item is explained in the account.
- **The account.** A written document that stands without you: the thesis itself; for a system, a paper of 5,000 to 8,000 words — problem, precedent, design, evaluation, failure modes, the role of machine intelligence; for a venture, a memo of the same length — what it does, who pays, how it survives, what the first users say, what is wrong with it. Frozen on Day 9, delivered to the examiners on Day 10, two weeks before the defense.
- **The defense.** Twenty minutes presenting, with the thing in the room, then an hour of questions from a panel of at least three: one faculty adviser, one Institute faculty from outside your concentration, and one outside examiner with a body of work in the field. Public: anyone may attend; it is recorded and published with the work unless the venture's confidentiality forbids it.
- **The report and corrections.** The examiners' written report within a week of the defense — pass, pass with corrections, or not yet — and your corrections, completed and signed off before graduation.

Day 1 fixes what finished means; Day 2 names the examiners; Days 3 to 8 finish and write; Day 9 freezes; Day 10 delivers; Days 11 and 12 rehearse; Day 13 is the defense; Day 14 the corrections; Day 15 the portfolio and the next work. The learner knows on Day 1 exactly what is due and when, because the calendar was fixed in CAP 401, Day 6.

## The unassisted examination

Three places, all by nature. Day 6, Week 6: the claim in 300 words, written in one sitting with the tools closed — what the work is, what it shows, how you know — read by the adviser beside the account's abstract; if they differ, the unaided one is the one examined. Day 13, Week 13: the defense itself — no notes beyond the slides, no device on the table, questions unseen. Day 14: the examiners' questions you could not answer, answered in writing within a week, unaided. The reason: this is the course in which the institution certifies the human, so the human is observed finishing, claiming, and answering alone.

## The checkpoints

**The three accepted forms.** A **research thesis**: a question nobody has answered, a method, evidence, an argument, written at the length the question needs, examined by someone who knows the field. **An engineered system that works**: software, hardware, or both, that does a real job for a real user, runs without you in the room, and has been measured doing it. **A venture that exists**: an organization legally formed, with users or customers who are not your friends, and a plain account of what it does and how it survives. All three are open to every concentration; a work that combines forms is examined as the one it leans on. The form was chosen in CAP 401 and does not change this semester.

**The fifteen weekly checkpoints of this semester.** Each is one hour with the adviser or the capstone section, with the week's document in hand.

| Week | Checkpoint | What exists at the end of the week |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Finished, defined | The proposal's "finished" list reread against the work; each item marked done, doable by Week 9, or cut with a reason. |
| 2 | The examiners | The panel named and confirmed: adviser, outside-concentration faculty, outside examiner. The outside examiner has agreed in writing. |
| 3 | The cut list | What is cut, in writing, and what finishing now requires week by week. |
| 4 | The world | The work meets someone outside: thesis data complete; the system deployed for its user; the venture's customers counted. |
| 5 | The account, draft one | The thesis, paper, or memo in full draft, however rough. |
| 6 | The claim | The unaided 300 words; the account's abstract revised to match. |
| 7 | Difficulties | The five hardest questions an examiner could ask, written by you, answered in the account. |
| 8 | Against the sequence | The HUM 402 presentation drafted: the three texts from four years the work answers, and how. |
| 9 | Freeze | The work and the account versioned, archived, and no longer changing. |
| 10 | Delivery | The account and access to the work sent to the examiners; the defense announced publicly. |
| 11 | The talk | The twenty minutes rehearsed in front of peers with the thing in the room. |
| 12 | The mock defense | A full hour of hostile questions from peers and faculty; what you could not answer, written down. |
| 13 | The defense | Held, in public, recorded. |
| 14 | The report and corrections | The examiners' report received; corrections listed, scheduled, begun; unanswered questions answered in writing. |
| 15 | The portfolio | Work, account, recording, report, corrections, and the four-year portfolio assembled; the next work named in one paragraph. |

**The learner alone online.** You have no faculty and no outside examiners assigned. Run the capstone as a self-set work with a found examiner, and hold a real defense. By Week 2 you need two examiners who are not your friends and not your adviser: one with a body of work in your form's field, one from well outside it — a working engineer and a working novelist, a founder and a scientist. Find them where people with bodies of work gather: a professional society, a university department that answers email, a meetup, a forum where your field's serious people post, the mentor from your Field Term. Send each a one-paragraph description of the work and ask for ninety minutes in Week 13 plus two weeks to read. Most people with a body of work remember their own defense and will say yes to a serious stranger. If you can pay them, pay them. Deliver the account two weeks before. Hold the defense on a recorded call or in a borrowed room — a library, a coworking space, a shop after hours — and invite the public, however small. Publish the recording and the account together. Ask the examiners for a written paragraph each: pass, pass with corrections, or not yet, and why. Do the corrections. Then it is defended, in the only sense that ever mattered: people who could not be fooled looked at it and said what they saw.

## Open questions for Brandon

1. The outside examiner's standing. The arc requires one examiner with a body of work in the field and no Institute affiliation. Does the Institute pay them, and does their written report carry a vote on pass/not-yet, or only advice to faculty?
2. Publication. The arc publishes the recording and the account with the work by default, with a confidentiality exception for ventures. Is default-public right for theses and systems, given Law 8?
3. "Not yet." What happens to a student whose work is judged not yet in Week 13? The arc assumes a second defense in the summer before graduation is possible. Confirm, or name the alternative.
