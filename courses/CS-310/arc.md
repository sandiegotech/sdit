# Arc — CS 310 Machine Learning II / AI Systems Engineering

## The course in one paragraph

At the start you can train a model and report a number. At the end you can put a model inside a system, give that system to people who are not you, measure it the same way before every change, watch what it does when nobody is looking, and write down honestly where it failed and why. The catalog register is applied machine learning systems — deployment, evaluation, agents, failure modes. The teaching frame is real systems in the world: what breaks, what lies, what scales. CS 220 taught you what a model is. This studio teaches you that the model is the smallest part.

## The made thing

A deployed AI system with real users, an evaluation harness, and a documented failure analysis. Concretely: a service with a model inside it, reachable at an address, used by at least ten people who are not in the course, for a job they would otherwise do another way. Beside it, an evaluation harness — a fixed set of cases with expected outcomes, run before every change, with the numbers kept over time. Beside that, a failure analysis written in the form of an accident report: what we believed, what was true, what we changed, what we still do not know. "Finished" means the system has run for at least four weeks in the hands of its users, the harness has caught at least one regression before it shipped, and the failure analysis names at least three failures found in the field rather than on the bench. It is defended on Day 15 in front of the room and in front of two of its users: the system runs live, the harness runs live, the failure analysis is read aloud.

Day 1 states the premise. Day 2 is the brief: you pick a job, a first user by name, and you ship the smallest true version before Day 5. Day 3 starts the harness. Day 5 is the first critique with the system live. By Day 10 it has tools, logs, and a monitor. Day 14 is the failure analysis. Day 15 is the defense.

## The unassisted examination

Day 11, tools closed. You are handed a page of evaluation results, a page of logs from a system you have never seen, and the system's one-paragraph description. In ninety minutes, by hand, you diagnose what went wrong, state what you would measure to confirm it, and write a one-page failure analysis in the form taught on Day 14 (which you will have read ahead; the form is in the brief). You also write, on paper, a working evaluation function for one stated case. Why this: the institution certifies the human, and in this field the human's job is to tell when the machine is wrong. A graduate who cannot read a log without an assistant is not an engineer of these systems; they are a user of them.

## The studio

**The brief.** Build a system with a model inside it that does one real job for real people. Candidates the studio has used or will use: a search-and-answer tool over a corpus people actually consult (the Institute's own course pages, a club's rulebook, a surf forecast archive); a triage assistant for a small organization's inbox; a transcription-and-summary tool for a weekly meeting; a classifier that watches a camera at a break and reports conditions. The job must be small enough to ship by Day 5 and real enough that someone will notice when it is wrong. The system must have: an address, a user who is not you, a harness, logs, a way to turn it off, and a written account of what it may never do.

**Technique days.** Day 3 (the harness), Day 4 (what the metric hides), Day 6 (confabulation and grounding), Day 7 (hostile input and the instruction boundary), Day 8 (drift and monitoring), Day 9 (agents, tools, and stopping), Day 12 (cost, latency, caching), Day 13 (designing the failure mode).

**Critique days.** Day 5, Day 10, Day 14. A critique is the room running your system, reading your logs, and trying to break it, with you taking notes. The rule: demonstrate one failure yourself before anyone else finds one.

**Milestone days.** Day 2 (brief accepted, first user named), Day 5 (live, three users, harness of fifty cases), Day 10 (tools and a monitor, ten users, harness of a hundred cases, one regression caught), Day 15 (defense).

**The build sequence.** By Day 5 you have a model behind an address, three users, and a harness you run by hand. By Day 10 the system can call at least one tool, logs every request, watches its inputs for drift, and the harness runs before every change without you remembering to run it. By Day 15 it has run for four weeks, its failure analysis is written, and two of its users are in the room.

**How the AI tools are used, and then closed.** The tools are open for everything: writing the service, writing the harness, generating candidate test cases, reading logs, drafting the failure analysis. The discipline is that every generated test case is checked by a human before it enters the harness, every generated diagnosis is confirmed against the logs, and on Day 11 you do the whole job by hand.

**What happens off the page.** The studio is a room with a master in it — someone who has shipped machine-learning systems to real users and been paged when they broke — and the page is the brief. In the room there is a weekly bench review where logs are read aloud, a shared status board, and a server you do not own. A learner alone online does the smallest real version: one model behind one address, one user who is not you, fifty test cases you wrote yourself, a log file, and a one-page failure analysis after two weeks. That is a real system. It is enough to learn everything this course teaches, at the scale of one.
