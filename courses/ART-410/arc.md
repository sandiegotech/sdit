# Arc — ART 410 Generative Media & Creative Code

## The course in one paragraph

At the start you can make a thing. At the end you can make a thing that makes things — author a rule set, run it, choose from what it produces, and defend the choosing — and you can do that with a machine-learning model as one material among others, without mistaking its output for your work. The catalog register is computational art: procedural and generative methods, AI-assisted media. The teaching frame is AI and procedure as artistic material — the newest medium, handled with taste. The artist's job moves up a level, to the rules and the taste; this course is where that sentence stops being a slogan and becomes a practice with a critique method. The page gives one rule-made thing to look at each day, from a snowflake to a 1704 tiling to a neural network playing death metal forever. The room gives the printer, the floor to lay the outputs on, and a master who has curated.

## The made thing

A generative work where the system is yours: the rules authored, the output curated, the taste defended. Concretely: one system — code, a written procedure, or a prompt-and-sampling architecture around a model — in one medium (image, sound, text, or motion), run to produce a body of at least a thousand outputs, curated to an exhibited set (a dozen prints, a ten-minute audio piece, a live piece, a page that regenerates), with the rules published beside the outputs — source code, or a procedure another person can execute — and a 200-word curatorial statement. "Finished" means: a classmate has run the system cold, without you in the room, and produced outputs that are recognizably the same work and not the same pictures; the exhibited set is chosen and sequenced; the rules are on the wall. The exhibition is the semester's public show. The defense is why these outputs, why this rule, what the machine made and what you did. The set, the rules, and the statement enter the portfolio and are read at the Year 4 gate; many of them become capstone proposals.

Day 1 introduces rules as the medium. Day 4 scopes the system: the rules written in one paragraph that day, before code. Days 5 to 7 are the technique days — noise, emergence, growth — applied to the learner's own system. Day 8 is the first critique on a thousand outputs. Day 12 freezes the rules. Day 15 is the exhibition. By Day 4 you know what you are making; by Day 8 it runs; by Day 15 it has been shown with the rules beside it.

## The unassisted examination

Day 10, in the room, tools closed. Two parts. First: write a generative procedure on paper that a classmate can execute by hand with dice, a deck of cards, and a pencil, in under an hour; the classmate executes it; you curate from what comes back and say why. Second: twenty outputs from a system you have never seen, and twenty minutes to infer and write the rule.

Why this: the author of rules must be able to author rules without a model. Reading a rule from its outputs is the core of generative taste — and the one thing a learner who has only prompted cannot do. The paper system also proves the course's claim from Day 2: Mozart needed a table and two dice.

## The studio

**The brief.** One system whose rules you author, one medium, one body of a thousand outputs, one curated set exhibited with the rules beside it. Written on Day 4 as a paragraph of rules before any code; the rules frozen and published on Day 12.

**Technique days.** 3 (procedure in a language a machine runs and one a person can), 5 (noise as material), 6 (local rules, global shape: automata, flocking, agents), 7 (rewriting and growth: L-systems, reaction–diffusion), 9 (the model as material: conditioning, seeds, sampling, selection), 13 (curation and sequencing), 14 (live systems and the audience as input). Each one is a method the learner adds to their own system that week.

**Critique days.** 8 (a thousand outputs on the floor: the room picks ten, the learner says which they would have picked and why; the worst ten are read for what they say about the rule), 11 (the authorship critique: where does your work begin, and could you prove it to a stranger), 12 (a classmate runs your system cold; if they cannot, it is not finished).

**Milestone days.** 4 (rules written), 8 (the system runs; a thousand outputs exist), 12 (rules frozen and published; reproducible by someone else), 15 (exhibited with the rules on the wall).

**Off the page.** The studio is a room with a master in it, and the page is the brief. The room holds fifteen people and one artist who has made and shown generative work — code-based, procedural, or model-based — and who has curated. It meets for one long block each week. In it: a large-format printer, a floor big enough to lay a hundred prints on, a projector, speakers, the machines that run the models, and a wall for the show. Harold Cohen built AARON up the road at UC San Diego for four decades and worked out of Encinitas until he died in 2016; the Harold Cohen Trust and the Whitney's 2024 retrospective are the local lineage, and a visit to the Trust's archive is the field trip. The AI tools are open as material every day and closed on Day 10. A learner on the open path runs p5.js in a browser, prints at a copy shop, and lays the outputs on a kitchen floor; the page says so.

3. **The exhibition form.** Prints on a wall are the default. If the semester's public show has a projector and speakers, live and audio pieces are equal citizens; if it is a hallway, they are not. Which room is the show in?
