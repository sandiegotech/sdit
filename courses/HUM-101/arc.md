# Arc — HUM 101 The Ancient World

## The course in one paragraph

At the start, the learner has heard of Homer and Plato and has read neither. At the end, they can open an ancient text cold, find its argument, say it back accurately in their own words, and carry it into a decision they are making this week. The catalog register is a survey of ancient Mediterranean literature and philosophy: Homer, the tragedians, Thucydides, Plato. The teaching frame is the Double Feature: each text travels with a film and an album, and the three are discussed as one conversation, so the learner also leaves able to defend a connection across three thousand years with evidence from all three sides. The spine of the course is one claim, planted on Day 1 and tested on Day 15: the Greeks were doing systems thinking about human weakness before anyone had a name for it, and their designs still work.

## The made thing

A made response to the ancient world, in one of four forms: an essay (1,200 to 2,000 words), a short film (under six minutes), a recorded piece (a song, a spoken track, a sound work), or a designed object (a thing you can hold, with a one-page note). Whatever the form, it must do one job: take one ancient design for a human weakness and show it working, or failing, in the present. "Finished" means it exists in a form someone else can experience without you in the room, it names its text, and it can survive five minutes of questions. It is defended in seminar in the week after Day 15: three minutes to present, seven of questions, from peers first and faculty second. It enters the portfolio and is read against Judgment, Articulation, and Breadth of Mind at the end-of-year gate.

Day 4 introduces it: Odysseus is recognized by a bed he built, and the course says plainly that a made thing is how you prove who you are. Day 9 scopes it: the learner picks one text and one weakness and writes a two-sentence brief. Day 12 starts it: a first draft, a rough cut, a demo, or a sketch exists by Day 13. Day 15 sets the defense format and sends the learner into the final week knowing exactly what they are making and what will be asked.

## The unassisted examination

In the week of Day 11, thirty minutes, tools closed, by hand or in a plain editor: restate the two positions of the Melian Dialogue in your own words, then say which position the chapter on the plague supports, and why. Thucydides is the right text for this because he is the course's test of honesty: can you report an argument you disagree with so accurately that its author would sign it? That skill is the one the tools most easily counterfeit, so it is the one the institution most needs to see unaided. The Day 15 defense is also unaided: the learner answers questions out loud, with nothing open.

## Open questions for Brandon

1. *Pet Sounds* as the Odyssey's album: the case is that it is the same year and coast as *The Endless Summer* and the voyage inward to the film's voyage outward. The alternative is *Graceland* (a pilgrimage record) if you want the album to travel too. Which?
2. *The Thin Red Line* is the war film for the *Iliad*. The Bible mentions "Book One of the Iliad against a war film" as a Perspective; if that Perspective is already planned around a different film, this unit should match it.
3. Unit 3 uses two tragedians (Sophocles for Day 8, Aeschylus for Day 9) so that the course reaches the invention of the jury, which HUM 201 picks up with Federalist 55 and *12 Angry Men*. If you would rather hold the unit to one play, Day 9 becomes the chorus at Epidaurus and the jury waits for the Enlightenment.
