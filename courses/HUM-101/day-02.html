Day 02 — My Name Is Nobody

Ten minutes on the page. One cave, one lie that works, one shout that undoes it.

Read

From today the course reads the Lectio edition of the Odyssey, the Institute’s own plain-English text; the voice changes from Day 1’s Butler. Odysseus is trapped in the cave with a giant who eats two of his men a night. He gets the giant drunk, and the giant asks his name.

“Cyclops, you ask my honored name, and I will tell it — and you give me the guest-gift you promised. Nobody is my name. Nobody my mother and father call me, and all my friends.”

The stake goes in. The giant roars for help, and when the other Cyclopes shout through the rock to ask who is hurting him, he answers: Nobody. They shrug and go home. The lie holds. The men escape under the sheep, reach the ship, and pull for open water. Then this:

“But they could not bend my stubborn heart, and I shouted back at him a second time, in my anger: ‘Cyclops, if ever any mortal man asks you who put out your eye and shamed you, tell him it was Odysseus, sacker of cities, the son of Laertes, whose home is in Ithaca!’”

One idea

The whole escape works because he withholds who he is. It nearly fails the moment he cannot.

Look at what the lie cost him: nothing. He gave up a name for one night and kept his life and his crew. Look at what the shout costs: the giant now has a name to curse, and he curses it to his father, Poseidon, who owns the sea Odysseus has to cross. The curse he screams after the ship is the rest of the book. Ten years of it.

Homer’s hero is the best problem-solver in the poem, and here the poem shows him failing the one discipline cunning depends on. Cunning is not cleverness. It is the ability to let a win go unsigned. Odysseus can out-think a giant; he cannot stand to be nobody for one more mile.

That is not an ancient weakness. It is the reflex to post the thing, name the spot, correct the record, take the credit, when the quiet version would have cost nothing and the loud one costs you the sea. The catalog calls this course a survey of ancient Mediterranean literature. The survey keeps turning up the same person, and it is you.

Carry the question

For the rest of today, carry this one:

When did needing credit for something cost you more than the thing was worth?

Answer in a line or a page, or not at all. What you write is kept in your Notebook.

That’s it. Day 2 is done.

If you’re still curious

Three doors. Open one, or none — they’ll be here tomorrow too.

Read the real thing. Book 9 in full, in the Lectio edition on the Lectio app, or free in Samuel Butler’s translation on Project Gutenberg. The Lotus-eaters come first, and they are the same lesson from the other side: forgetting who you are is not the cure for needing everyone to know.

Watch the other half. The Endless Summer (1966), the film this course pairs with the Odyssey. Two surfers cross the world looking for a wave nobody has ridden, and the film’s quietest moment is the one where they decide not to say where it is.

Ask someone. Tonight, ask a surfer or a skater: “Do you tell people about your best spot?” Listen for the pause before the answer. The pause is Odysseus on the ship, deciding.
