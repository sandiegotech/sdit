# Arc — CAP 401 Thesis / Venture Capstone I

## The course in one paragraph

At the start you have three years of made work and a question you have been circling without naming. At the end you have named it: a one-page proposal, accepted, for one original work in one of three forms — a research thesis, an engineered system that works, or a venture that exists — and the first real piece of that work, made and shown. The catalog register is Capstone I: proposal and scoping of an original work, with execution begun under faculty advising. The teaching frame is the three accepted forms, and this semester the proposal, the scope, and the first real work. This is not a subject course. Nothing here is taught; the fifteen days are fifteen weekly checkpoints on a year-long work, each one a place to stand and look at what you have made since last week, with one document, photograph, or passage from someone who did the thing, and one question to carry into the work. Semester 7 begins the year; Semester 8 (CAP 402) finishes and defends it. HUM 402 is where the work is argued against everything you have read.

## The made thing

Two things this semester: **an accepted capstone proposal** and **the first working piece of the thing itself**.

- The proposal is one page plus appendices. The page says: the form (thesis, system, or venture); the claim or the need, in one sentence; what "finished" means, as a list the examiners can check; what is out of scope; the precedents and what they leave undone; how machine intelligence will be used and how it will be verified; the calendar to the defense date; and the adviser's signature. Appendices hold the precedent survey, the risk list, and the plan. "Accepted" means the adviser and one second reader have signed it after an oral scoping conversation (Day 7). Scope can change later; the date cannot.
- The first working piece depends on the form. For a thesis: one chapter with real data or a complete argument, and the method it rests on. For a system: it runs end to end, badly, on a real input, in front of someone. For a venture: it is incorporated, or the equivalent legal fact exists, and one person who is not your friend has used it or paid for it. "Finished" for the piece means: shown to peers and the adviser in Week 14, dated, and in the record.

Day 1 introduces the work; Days 2 to 5 choose and scope it; Day 7 accepts the proposal; Day 8 starts the piece; Day 14 shows it. By Day 15 the learner knows exactly what they are making, has an accepted page that says so, and has a piece that exists.

## The unassisted examination

Day 7, Week 7: the proposal's one page is written in one sitting with the tools closed, before the oral scoping conversation, and read beside the machine-assisted draft. If the two pages disagree, the unaided one is the one that is true. Then the oral conversation itself: thirty minutes with the adviser and the second reader, no notes, where you answer what the work is, why it, why you, and how you will know it is done. Day 12, Week 12: a one-page "state of the work," also tools closed. The reason: the capstone is the terminal demonstration of all five outcomes, and the first of them is Judgment. The institution has to watch you judge your own work without help.

## The checkpoints

**The three accepted forms.** A **research thesis**: a question nobody has answered, a method, evidence, and an argument, written at the length the question needs, examined by someone who knows the field. **An engineered system that works**: software, hardware, or both, that does a real job for a real user, runs without you in the room, and has been measured doing it. **A venture that exists**: an organization — company, cooperative, nonprofit, label, studio — legally formed, with users or customers who are not your friends, and a plain account of what it does and how it survives. Media capstones lean toward works that move people and are usually theses-in-practice (a film, a record, an interactive piece, with the written account beside them) or ventures; Design capstones toward products in real hands; Engineering capstones toward systems that run. All three forms are open to every concentration. A work may combine forms; it is examined as the one it leans on.

**The fifteen weekly checkpoints of this semester.** Each is one hour with the adviser or the capstone section, with the week's document in hand.

| Week | Checkpoint | What exists at the end of the week |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The circling question | One paragraph naming what you have been returning to for three years, with the portfolio pieces that prove it. |
| 2 | The form | A choice among the three forms, argued in half a page, with the examiner it implies. |
| 3 | Precedent | A survey of what already exists: five to twenty works, each in two lines, and the gap they leave. |
| 4 | The page | The one-page proposal, draft one. |
| 5 | Scope | The "finished" list and the "not this" list; the hardest part named and kept. |
| 6 | The calendar | Every week to the defense date, with the defense date fixed and the examiners' delivery date two weeks before it. |
| 7 | Acceptance | The unaided one page; the oral scoping conversation; two signatures. |
| 8 | The first dated piece | Something real, small, dated, in the record. |
| 9 | First contact | A first reader, a first run on real input, or a first user — the work has touched someone outside you. |
| 10 | Evidence | A test, repeated; the measurement an examiner who does not like you would accept. |
| 11 | The delegation log | What machine intelligence did this semester, what you checked, what it got wrong. |
| 12 | State of the work | The unaided one page: what is true about the work today; the adviser's cut-or-keep review. |
| 13 | The record | The log, notebook, or repository an examiner could read cold; entries dated the day they happened. |
| 14 | The piece, shown | The first working piece demonstrated to peers and adviser; written feedback kept. |
| 15 | The handoff to spring | The plan for CAP 402 revised; the three texts the work will be argued against in HUM 402 named; the break's work scheduled. |

**The learner alone online.** You have no adviser assigned and no section to meet. Run the capstone as a self-set work with a found examiner. The forms are the same three; pick one and hold yourself to its definition of "exists." Find an adviser by Week 4: one person with a body of work in your form's field who will read a page a week — a former teacher, someone from a meetup or a forum who has done the thing, a working professional who answers email. Pay them if you can; most will do it for the page. Find your second reader by Week 7. Write the proposal, send it, and have the scoping conversation on a call. Record it. Keep the calendar: the defense date is a real date on a real calendar, and CAP 402 explains how the examiners are found and the defense is held. The unaided pages are written on paper or in a plain editor with the network off, and you say so on them. The standard is the same: a page that is accepted, a piece that exists.

## Open questions for Brandon

1. The second reader. The arc requires a second signature on the proposal (Day 7). Should the second reader be the outside examiner who will sit at the defense, so they meet the work early, or someone else, so the examiner meets it cold?
2. The venture form and money. "Incorporated, with users" implies filing fees and possibly revenue. Does the Institute hold the entity, fund the filing, or stay out of it? The arc assumes the student owns the venture outright.
3. Media capstones. The arc treats a film or record as a thesis-in-practice with a written account beside it. Is that the intended reading of "works that move people," or should a fourth form be named?
