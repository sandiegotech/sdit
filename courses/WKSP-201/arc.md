# Arc — WKSP 201 The Workshop

## The course in one paragraph

At the start you have built two things for yourself and shown one to strangers; by the end you have built a working piece of audio electronics — a pedal, a preamp, a small amplifier — for a musician who is not you, on their brief, to their deadline, and handed it over with a schematic they can read and an honest account of what it cost. The catalog register is the third course of the eight-semester fabrication and design practicum; the teaching frame is that external clients begin: the team ships for someone else. The standard does not change. The person you answer to does.

## The made thing

A client project, shipped and documented — one piece of amplifier or pedal electronics that a named musician plugs in and uses. Finished means: it passes signal cleanly, does the one thing the client asked for, is boxed so the client touches knobs and not circuits, survives being stepped on or carried, and leaves with a one-page card (what it does, how to power it, what to do when it hums). Day 1 names the client and the brief; Day 3 writes the brief down in the client's words with a done-test; Day 5 has the circuit breathing on a breadboard and the client hearing it. By Day 15 the thing is in the client's hands, the schematic and the account are in the log, and what the client said is the last entry.

## The unassisted examination

Day 11's week, tools closed: solder three joints the examiner picks and have them pass a tug and a meter; then, from a schematic you have not seen, trace the signal path from input jack to output jack aloud and name what each stage does. Why: a pedal can be assembled from a kit by following pictures. The Workshop certifies that you can join metal and read a map of electrons without a machine telling you what either means.

## The build and the rotation

**This semester's material.** Amplifier and pedal electronics — the second tradition of the rotation. Teams build one of: a boost or fuzz pedal (one to three transistors), a two-knob overdrive, a passive or buffered DI, a small single-ended tube or solid-state practice amp. Through-hole parts, hand-wired or on perfboard. Kits are allowed as a starting schematic; the client's brief changes the design, so the kit is not the build.

**The client.** A musician — a student in SIG 101 last spring, a local band, a teacher, anyone who will actually plug it in and gig with it. The client comes to two crits (Day 7 and Day 13), signs off on the brief (Day 3), and receives the thing (Day 13). Online, the client is any musician you know who agrees to use it.

**The critique method.** Describe before you judge, with the client at the bench on Day 7. The client goes first and describes what they hear, not what they like. Then the room. Then the maker, last. From this semester the crit has a fourth round: the client's ears outrank the maker's intentions.

**The exhibition.** None this semester; the deliverable is the handover. Shipped means the client has it. The log and the schematic enter the portfolio for the Year 2 gate.

**The documentation discipline.** Three photos and a sentence, continuing the log. Added: the brief in the client's words (Day 3), the schematic as built, not as copied (Day 9), the test notes (Day 8, Day 10), the client card (Day 13), and the account of hours and parts (Day 14).

**The rotation across the eight semesters.**

1. **WKSP 101** (Semester 1) — Make anything, and finish it. No assigned tradition; the room's stock pulls most first builds toward the small end of board construction.
2. **WKSP 102** (Semester 2) — Surfboard and skateboard construction. The board goes to the year-end public exhibition.
3. **WKSP 201** (Semester 3) — Amplifier and pedal electronics. The first external client: a musician who will plug it in.
4. **WKSP 202** (Semester 4) — Motorcycle mechanics. A client's machine brought back to running; the last Foundation build and the evidence for the Declaration.
5. **WKSP 301** (Semester 5) — Camera rigs and housings. A client who needs a shot they cannot get; each concentration enters the tradition through its own materials.
6. **WKSP 302** (Semester 6) — Drones and ocean sensors, the sensor end. One instrument that survives a week in saltwater and comes back with data; lighter on purpose, beside the Field Term.
7. **WKSP 401** (Semester 7) — The first-years' build. You lead a WKSP 101 team; the material is whatever they can finish.
8. **WKSP 402** (Semester 8) — Your choice from the five traditions, including the drone end the sixth semester left open. The final build, exhibited beside the capstone.

**What the page is, honestly.** The Workshop is a bench with an iron, a meter, a scope, a parts drawer, and someone who has built a hundred of these. The page cannot be that. The fifteen days carry the brief, the technique in order, the critique prompt, and the discipline of the schematic and the log. A learner working alone online does the smallest real version: a one-transistor booster on a breadboard, then in a box, for a musician they know, who uses it at one rehearsal and tells them what they heard.

## Open questions for Brandon

1. Who is the first client? The arc assumes a musician found through SIG 101 or the local scene; if the Institute wants a standing relationship — a venue, a label, a school music program — Day 1 should name it.
2. Tube amps put lethal voltages on the bench. Should Semester 3 be pedals and solid-state only, with tubes held for a student who has passed a named safety check — or is the master in the room enough?
3. Should the account on Day 14 be shown to the client? It teaches the student what the thing cost; it may also teach the client to expect to pay, which the Institute may or may not want from a first-client semester.
