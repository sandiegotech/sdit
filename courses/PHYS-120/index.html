PHYS 120 — Waves, Energy & Fluids

Mathematics & Science · 4 units · Semester 2 · Required core — everyone takes it

Overview

Why Black’s breaks, why fins hold, why the marine layer burns off at eleven. This course is the physics of the place. Two submarine canyons bend swell onto one beach and starve its neighbor; a south swell wraps Point Loma into Ocean Beach; the fog forms over cold water, sits under a warm cap, and lifts when the ground has heated enough to mix it out; the afternoon wind is a heat engine running between hot land and cold sea. Waves, heat, and fluids are one subject here, because on this coast they are one weather.

Taught as Why Waves Break: swell generation, refraction at Point Loma, bathymetric focusing at Black’s, board hydrodynamics and fin theory, marine-layer thermodynamics. Every law is met in the water or the air before it is written — you count the period at Scripps Pier before you see the wave equation, feel the pressure in your ears at the Cove before you see Archimedes, hold your hand out the car window before you see lift. The lab is the beach, the buoy, and the shaping bay.

One day a week, about ten minutes a sitting, building toward real measurement, honest uncertainty, and a board. The course ends the way every SDIT course ends: in a made thing — a surfboard you shaped, with design calculations you can defend — exhibited, defended, and ridden.

The Two Registers

  • Catalog register: Wave mechanics, thermodynamics, and fluid dynamics; laboratory. Transfer-legible second-semester introductory physics.
  • Teaching frame: Why Waves Break — swell generation, refraction at Point Loma, why Black’s breaks (bathymetric focusing); board hydrodynamics and fin theory; marine-layer thermodynamics.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course you can:

  1. Describe a wave by its period, wavelength, frequency, and speed from your own observation; apply the deep- and shallow-water speed rules; and explain dispersion, superposition, refraction, shoaling, and resonance in a real sea. → Judgment
  2. Distinguish heat, temperature, and specific heat; trace heat transfer in a real case; and bound what a heat engine can do, using the sea breeze and the marine layer as the cases. → Judgment
  3. Apply pressure with depth, Archimedes’ principle, continuity, Bernoulli’s principle, and drag scaling to a surfboard, a fin, and a rip current, with numbers. → Workmanship
  4. Measure something real at the pier, the buoy, or the bay, estimate its uncertainty, and say honestly what the data can and cannot support. → Judgment · Workmanship
  5. Demonstrate the above unassisted, in periodic written examinations. → Judgment

Each outcome maps to the five institutional outcomes (Judgment, Command of Intelligence, Workmanship, Articulation, Breadth of Mind) — the assessment spine of the degree and its accreditation evidence file.

The Made Thing

A shaped board with design calculations, defended. The board is shaped in the Workshop’s surfboard rotation; this course owns the numbers. For a named rider and a named wave you compute the wave’s speed at the break and so the speed the board must reach, the volume in liters by slicing the blank and the buoyancy margin for the rider, the planing trade-off that rocker and width make, the fin area and angle that hold the rail at bottom-turn speed, and the drag budget. The board exists; a two-page design sheet sits beside it with every number sourced. The room changes one thing — heavier rider, smaller wave, bigger fin — and you say which numbers move and which way. It is exhibited at the Semester 2 public exhibition and ridden.

How You’re Assessed

  • Lab work and notebooks — measurement, uncertainty, and reproducibility are graded, not just answers.
  • The board and its design sheet, exhibited and defended.
  • Unassisted written examinations — waves and thermodynamics after the first half; fluids after the second. The proportions and pictures that must live in your head on the sand, with the tools closed.
  • Everything lands in your portfolio, reviewed against the five outcomes at the end-of-year gate.
