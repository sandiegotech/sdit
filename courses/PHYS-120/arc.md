# Arc — PHYS 120 Waves, Energy & Fluids

## The course in one paragraph

At the start, the learner knows Black's is better than La Jolla Shores on the same swell and cannot say why, has felt the marine layer burn off and the afternoon wind come up and taken both as weather. At the end, the learner can read a buoy spectrum and say which swell arrives when and where it will focus; explain the bend at Point Loma, the canyon under Black's, the sets and the lulls; say why the fog forms, why it lifts at eleven, and why the sea breeze is a heat engine; and compute a surfboard's volume, buoyancy, planing speed, and fin lift well enough to shape one and defend the numbers. That is the catalog register, wave mechanics, thermodynamics, and fluid dynamics, with laboratory. The teaching frame is the place itself — swell generation, refraction at Point Loma, bathymetric focusing at Black's, board hydrodynamics and fin theory, marine-layer thermodynamics — and every law is met in the water or the air before it is written.

## The made thing

A shaped board with design calculations, defended. The board is shaped in WKSP 102's surfboard rotation; this course owns the numbers. For a named rider and a named wave (a spot, a swell size, a period), the learner computes and defends: the wave's speed at the break from its depth, and so the paddle speed and planing speed the board must reach; the volume in liters by slicing the blank at stations (with MATH 120), and the buoyancy margin for the rider's weight; the wetted area and the planing trade-off that rocker and width make; the fin area, foil, and angle of attack that hold the rail at the bottom-turn speed, with the lift estimated and the tip vortex named; and the drag budget, where it goes and what glass versus chop does to it. "Finished" means the board exists, a two-page design sheet sits beside it with every number sourced, and the learner answers one change from the room (heavier rider, smaller wave, bigger fin) by saying which numbers move and which way. It is defended on Day 15, exhibited at the Semester 2 public exhibition, and ridden.

Day 1 introduces it (you will shape a board and the numbers will be yours). Day 6 scopes it (name the rider and the wave; compute the wave's speed at the break). Day 11 starts it (volume and buoyancy from the blank). Days 13 and 14 add fins and drag. Day 15 is the defense.

## The unassisted examination

Two sittings, tools closed. After Day 10: wavelength, period, frequency, and speed from a counted observation; the deep- and shallow-water speed rules and what each predicts; superposition and a standing wave sketched; heat, temperature, and specific heat distinguished in a real case; a heat engine's efficiency bounded. After Day 14: pressure with depth, Archimedes applied to a board and a rider, continuity through a rip channel, Bernoulli across a fin, and the direction a Reynolds number points. These are examined unaided because they are proportions and pictures that must live in the head when the learner is standing on the sand reading a wave, where there is no machine.
