# Arc — CS 210 Data Structures & Algorithms

## The course in one paragraph

At the start you can write a program that works. At the end you can write one that still works when the data is a thousand times bigger, and you can say — with a stopwatch and with a proof — why. The catalog register is data structures, algorithm design and analysis, and complexity: lists, stacks, queues, hash tables, trees, graphs; searching and sorting; growth rates and the notation for them. The teaching frame is Making Things Fast: the classic course, taught against real performance problems you can measure. Every structure arrives as a physical thing first (a phone book, a border queue, a card catalog, a bridge map), then as a measurement on your own data, and only then as notation. You leave able to look at slow code, know why it is slow, make it fast, and prove the speedup is real.

## The made thing

A measurably faster program — profiled, redesigned, and benchmarked, with the analysis to show why. The program is your own: the default is a question-answering tool over a real dataset you choose on Day 5. The course's suggested dataset is the Coastal Data Information Program archive (CDIP, Scripps Institution of Oceanography, public): decades of half-hourly wave height, period, and direction from the buoys off Point Loma, Torrey Pines, and Oceanside. Questions like "the ten biggest days at Blacks since 2000" or "how often does a three-second period gap precede a size jump" are slow to answer naively and fast to answer well. Alternatives: your own CS 110 program, now fed a thousand times more data; the San Diego MTS transit feed (open GTFS) for shortest routes; your own photo library or message export. "Finished" means five things ship: the slow version, timed; the fast version, timed, on the same inputs; a chart of running time against input size for both; a written argument for why the growth rates differ, in the notation; and a profile showing where the time went before and after. It is defended by demonstration — both versions run live on the largest input — and by questioning on the proof.

Day 1 gives you the stopwatch. Day 5 picks the dataset and times the honest slow version. Day 10 has the redesign running. Day 15 ships. The learner knows the question they are answering by Day 5 and has a baseline measured the same day.

## The unassisted examination

Twice, tools closed, on paper. **Day 8:** given three short code fragments, state how each one's running time grows with input size, and say why; then trace binary search by hand on a sorted list of fifteen numbers, writing down every comparison. **Day 14:** write binary search from memory, correctly, including the edge cases; read a real profiler output printed on the page and say where the time is going; and give the growth-rate argument for one sort. These are examined because they are the irreducible core: a person who cannot say how a loop's cost grows cannot judge whether the assistant's "optimization" is one, and a person who cannot write binary search by hand has only memorized its name. Pass or revise; repeated at the annual gate.

## The build sequence

**Day 5:** a timing harness — ten lines that run a function on inputs of size n, 2n, 4n, 8n and print the seconds — wrapped around the honest slow version of your program on real data. A table of times. The ratio between rows tells you the shape of the cost before you have a name for it.

**Day 10:** the redesign. The same question answered with the right structure — a dictionary where you were scanning a list, a sort-once-then-binary-search where you were searching repeatedly, a heap where you kept finding the maximum — and the same harness showing the new numbers beside the old. A chart. The assistant proposed at least one change you measured and rejected because it did not help.

**Day 15:** both versions, the profile before and after, the chart, the written proof, defended.

**How the AI tools are used:** open from Day 1. The assistant is good at suggesting the right structure and at writing it; it is also confident about running times it has not measured. The course's discipline is that every claim of speed is a measurement, and every measurement is yours. Days 6, 10, and 13 ask you to benchmark the assistant's claims, including the one that turns out false. **How the human is examined unaided:** Days 8 and 14, as above; and the standing rule that any line of the fast version may be asked about, including "why is this faster?", with the answer given without a tool.

## Open questions for Brandon

1. **CDIP as the default dataset.** It is the most San Diego choice and the data is public, but it ties the course to one domain. Should it be the default, with alternatives, or should every learner be required to use their own data?
2. **Notation timing.** The arc withholds big-O names until Day 8, after six days of measured shapes. That is Law 3 taken strictly. Is it too late for transfer-legibility, or exactly right?
3. **A master for Day 10 or 15.** A performance engineer — someone who has made a real system faster under load — for the "ask someone" door and, ideally, to sit on the Day 15 defense. Do you know one?
