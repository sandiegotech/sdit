# MATH 120 — Calculus II

**Mathematics & Science** · 4 units · Semester 2 · Required core — everyone takes it

## Overview

Every sound you have ever loved is a sum of sines, and the synthesizer is the lab bench where you prove it. Calculus I zoomed in; this course stitches back together. An integral is the total of a changing thing — the swing of a VU meter, the liters in a surfboard blank, the length of a reverb tail — and an infinite series is a sum that never stops but can still land somewhere, like the echoes of a delay pedal with the feedback under one hundred percent. By the end, Fourier's theorem is not a formula but a thing you have heard: any repeating wave taken apart into the sines it is made of, and put back together with the coefficients you computed.

Taught as **The Mathematics of Sound**: harmonics and Fourier through synthesizers and the studio. Every concept arrives physical before formal — you hear the square wave before you see the series, measure the blank before you write the integral, play the echo before you test the convergence. The notation is the last step, never the first.

One day a week, about ten minutes a sitting, with problems that start in the studio and end on the page. AI tools stay open for exploration; your own unassisted command of integration and series is examined, because the degree certifies you, not your tools. The course ends the way every SDIT course ends: in a made thing — a sound built from first principles — played and defended.

## The Two Registers

- **Catalog register:** Integral calculus and series — integration, applications, infinite series, Fourier introduction. Transfer-legible Calculus II.
- **Teaching frame:** The Mathematics of Sound — harmonics and Fourier through synthesizers and the studio. Every integral heard before it is written.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course you can:

1. Evaluate definite and indefinite integrals by substitution, by parts, and as improper integrals, and check a machine's answer by hand. → *Workmanship*
2. Set up and compute areas, volumes by slicing, arc lengths, work, and average and RMS values from physical situations, and defend the setup. → *Judgment · Workmanship*
3. Decide whether an infinite series converges, name the test, and say what the sum means physically. → *Judgment*
4. Compute Taylor and Fourier coefficients for a given function or waveform, and build the waveform from them on a synthesizer. → *Workmanship*
5. Demonstrate the above unassisted, in periodic written examinations. → *Judgment*

*Each outcome maps to the five institutional outcomes (Judgment, Command of Intelligence, Workmanship, Articulation, Breadth of Mind) — the assessment spine of the degree and its accreditation evidence file.*

## The Made Thing

A sound built from first principles — a patch or a short piece whose harmonic structure you can derive and defend. You choose a target sound, compute its Fourier coefficients by hand for one period, build it by adding sines at those amplitudes, shape it with an envelope whose loudness over time is an integral you have evaluated, and run it through a delay whose echoes are a geometric series you have summed. The sound plays; the derivation sits beside it; a spectrogram of the result lies over your prediction. It is defended in seminar and shown at the Semester 2 exhibition.

## How You're Assessed

- **Weekly problem sets** — AI tools open; what's graded is your setup, your verification, and your explanation.
- **The sound**, played and defended: the room names a harmonic, you name the coefficient.
- **Unassisted written examinations** — integration technique after the first half; series, Taylor, and Fourier coefficients after the second. AI-native, not AI-dependent.
- Everything lands in your **portfolio**, reviewed against the five outcomes at the end-of-year gate.
