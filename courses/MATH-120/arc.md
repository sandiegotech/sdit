# Arc — MATH 120 Calculus II

## The course in one paragraph

At the start, the learner can zoom in (Calculus I) and knows that a synthesizer makes sounds without knowing what a sound is. At the end, the learner can stitch back together: add up a changing thing over time to get a total, find a volume by slicing, say how loud a sound really is, sum an infinite string of echoes and know whether the sum is finite, build a sine out of a polynomial, and take any periodic sound apart into the sines it is made of, with the coefficients computed, and put it back together on a synthesizer. That is the catalog register, integral calculus and series: integration, applications, infinite series, Fourier introduction. The teaching frame is sound — harmonics and Fourier through synthesizers and the studio — and every integral is heard before it is written.

## The made thing

A sound built from first principles: a patch or a short piece whose harmonic structure the learner can derive and defend. The learner chooses a target — a square wave, a clarinet-like tone, a bell, a bass with a particular bite — and builds it by addition: sines at computed frequencies and computed amplitudes (the Fourier coefficients, worked by hand for one period), shaped by an envelope whose loudness over time is an integral the learner has evaluated, run through a delay whose echoes are a geometric series the learner has summed. "Finished" means the sound plays, a one-page derivation sits beside it (the integrals, the series, the coefficients), and a spectrogram of the result is laid over the prediction. It is played and defended in seminar on Day 15: the room names a harmonic, and the learner says which coefficient to change and by how much. It enters the portfolio and the WKSP 102 exhibition.

Day 1 introduces it (every sound is a sum; you will build one). Day 8 scopes it (choose the sound; measure its loudness as an average of a square). Day 12 starts it in earnest (compute the first six coefficients and dial them in). Day 15 is the defense.

## The unassisted examination

Two sittings, tools closed. After Day 9: definite integrals by substitution and by parts, an area and a volume by slicing, an average value, and one improper integral, on functions the learner has not seen. After Day 14: the convergence or divergence of four series with the test named, the first four terms of a Taylor series, and the first three Fourier coefficients of a simple wave. Integration technique is examined unaided because the learner must be able to check what a machine returns; convergence is examined unaided because knowing whether a sum is finite is a judgment, and the machine will happily print a number for a divergent one.

| Area between curves | Two waveforms against each other: the stereo difference; what cancels when a signal meets its own inverse | A mid/side meter |
| Volumes by slicing | A surfboard blank measured at ten stations and summed; a speaker cone; a stack of coins | The WKSP 102 shaping bay; the PHYS 120 board |
| Arc length | The length of a vinyl groove unrolled; the length of a wave face from lip to trough | A record on a turntable; Black's |
| Average value, RMS | How loud a sound really is: the mean of its square, then the root; why a square wave at the same peak is louder than a sine | Any meter in the studio |
| Work | Pushing a speaker cone against its spring; pumping water to a tank | A driver on the bench |
| Improper integrals | A reverb tail that never ends but has finite total energy; a decaying string | A cathedral recording; a struck guitar string |
| Sequences and geometric series | Delay feedback: each echo a fixed fraction of the last; the sum is finite when the feedback is under 100 percent and runs away when it is not | A tape echo or a delay pedal |
| Harmonic series and convergence tests | The overtones of a string have wavelengths 1, 1/2, 1/3, …; the sum of those lengths never settles; the series named after harmony diverges | A guitar string touched at the twelfth, seventh, and fifth frets |
| Taylor series | A synthesizer's "sine" is a polynomial; a pendulum is a sine only for small swings | A small-angle swing; a chip's sine lookup |
| Fourier series | Additive synthesis: drawbars on an organ; a square wave from odd harmonics; the ear hears the sum as one tone | A Hammond or any additive synth; the pipe organ at Spreckels, Balboa Park |
| Fourier transform, introduction | The spectrogram: time along, frequency up, loudness as color; the cochlea does this with hairs of different lengths | A free spectrogram app on a phone, held up to the surf |
