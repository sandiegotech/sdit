# Arc — ART 320 Film & the Moving Image

## The course in one paragraph

At the start you can point a camera at something and record it. At the end you can decide where to stand, why to cut, what the audience should hear, and say in one sentence what the film is for — and you have sat in the dark with strangers while they watched it. The catalog register is film production: cinematography, editing, sound, narrative. The teaching frame is camera, edit, story — you ship a short film, start to finish. The fifteen days run the whole chain once, on one film, with a public-domain century of cinema as the thing to look at each day: the Lumières, Méliès, Weber, Keaton, Eisenstein, Vertov, Murnau, Hitchcock, Ulmer. The page is the brief and the field guide. The room is where the footage gets watched cold.

## The made thing

A finished short film, three to eight minutes, with picture locked, sound mixed, color graded, titled, and delivered to a venue's specification — and screened in public to an audience that mostly does not know you. "Finished" means: a delivered master in two places; a shot list and a cut history that show how the film changed; a 150-word director's statement; a logline; and a screening date that happened. The screening is the exhibition and the defense: the room watches, then the learner answers questions from the audience and the master. The master file, the statement, and the cut history enter the portfolio and are read at the Year 3 gate.

Day 1 introduces the medium as a decision about where to stand. Day 4 scopes it: the brief and the logline are written that day, around what the learner already has access to. Day 5 to Day 7 are the technique days that prepare the shoot; principal photography happens off the page between Day 7 and Day 8. Day 8 is dailies. Day 12 is picture lock. Day 15 is the screening. By Day 4 you know what you are making; by Day 8 it is shot; by Day 15 it has been seen.

## The unassisted examination

Day 9, in the room, tools closed: ninety minutes with twenty minutes of footage the learner has never seen, an editing application and nothing else — no AI assistants, no plug-ins beyond the cut, no reference. Cut a sixty-second scene, then defend it cut by cut to the room. On Day 14 the director's statement is written in the room by hand, from the logline, before any draft is touched by a model.

Why this: the cut is the one act in filmmaking that is pure judgment with no equipment to hide behind, and it is the act most readily handed to a machine. The institution certifies the human, so the human cuts alone once, on camera, and explains.

## The studio

**The brief.** One short film, three to eight minutes, one location the learner can return to, one or two people who will show up, shot on the camera the learner has. Finished and screened by Day 15. Written on Day 4 as a logline and a paragraph; revised in writing at every milestone, with the revisions kept.

**Technique days.** 2 (the cut and the join), 3 (montage and the reaction shot), 5 (light: direction, size, distance), 6 (blocking and the frame), 7 (the shot list and coverage), 9 (editing rhythm), 10 (location sound and the meaning of sound), 11 (the sound edit: dialogue, effects, ambience, music), 13 (color, titles, delivery). Each one is applied to the learner's own film that week.

**Critique days.** 8 (dailies: the room watches all the footage cold and silent, then says what it saw), 12 (the rough cut screened start to finish, no pausing, then notes in a fixed order — story, then emotion, then rhythm), 14 (the statement and the logline read aloud to the room; the room says what film it expects to see).

**Milestone days.** 4 (brief and logline), 8 (photography complete), 12 (picture lock), 15 (screening).

**Off the page.** The studio is a room with a master in it, and the page is the brief. The room holds fifteen people and one working filmmaker — a cinematographer, editor, or director with credits — meeting for one long block each week, with a second block for the shoot weeks. In the room: the camera and sound kit for checkout, two edit stations with calibrated monitors, a sound booth, and the screening wall. Off the page the Institute books the venue (La Paloma in Encinitas or the Digital Gym Cinema) and the date before Day 4, so the deadline is real. The marine layer is the lighting department. The AI tools are open for transcription, rough selects, storyboards, and temp music, and closed for the Day 9 cut and the Day 14 statement. A learner on the open path shoots on a phone, cuts on free software, and screens for ten people in a living room with the lights off; the page says so.

| 1 | Where You Stand Is the Film | Auguste and Louis Lumière, *L'Arrivée d'un train en gare de La Ciotat*, 1896 (Internet Archive · public domain; embed) | Fifty seconds, one shot, no cut. | 2 | Two Shots Make a Third Thing | Georges Méliès, *Le Voyage dans la Lune*, 1902 (Internet Archive · public domain; the black-and-white print, not the 2011 color restoration) | Méliès said his camera jammed on a Paris street and a bus turned into a hearse: the cut was born as an accident. Two shots joined make a meaning neither held alone. Film's grammar is the join. | Count the cuts in one minute of whatever you watch tonight. Then ask what each one did. | read: Méliès on the stop trick, in his own account of 1907 (public domain; translation to confirm on Wikisource) · watch: Edwin S. | 3 | The Steps | Sergei Eisenstein, *Battleship Potemkin*, the Odessa Steps sequence, 1925 (Internet Archive · public domain) | Montage: meaning lives between the shots, and the audience makes the film in their heads. Kuleshov tested it; Eisenstein built a cathedral out of it; you will use it by Day 9. | Tonight, notice one reaction shot and ask what it made you feel that the screen never showed. | read: Eisenstein, "A Dialectic Approach to Film Form" (in *Film Form*; buy or borrow) · watch: Hitchcock explains the Kuleshov effect (CBC *Telescope*, 1964; link out) · ask: anyone: "Tell me a film moment you remember. | 4 | The Brief | Buster Keaton, *One Week*, 1920 (Internet Archive · public domain) | Nineteen minutes, one house, one week. | 5 | Light Is the Subject | F. W. | 6 | The Frame Is a Sentence | Every Frame a Painting, "Akira Kurosawa – Composing Movement" (Tony Zhou and Taylor Ramos; official channel, embeddable) | Blocking — where people stand and when they move — is writing. The lens you choose decides how close the audience sits. | Watch where two people stand during a conversation today, and say what the distance means. | read: Steven Katz, *Film Directing Shot by Shot* (buy or borrow) · watch: Buster Keaton, *Sherlock Jr.*, 1924 (Internet Archive) — the frame inside the frame · ask: a theater director: "How do you decide where someone stands?" |
| 7 | The Shot List Is a Promise | Georges Méliès, set and costume drawings for his films (Wikimedia Commons · public domain) — the plan before the shot | A shot list is a promise to tomorrow. It frees you to improvise because the necessary is already covered: wide, medium, close, insert, and the one shot that is the reason you came. Shoot the boring coverage first. Photography happens this week. | Plan tomorrow morning as five shots. Which one is the reason? | read: Truffaut, *Hitchcock* (buy or borrow) · watch: StudioBinder, "How to Make a Shot List" (official channel) · ask: an assistant director or a wedding photographer: "What goes wrong on the day?" |
| 8 | Dailies | Lois Weber, *Suspense*, 1913 (Internet Archive · public domain) — ten minutes, a split screen, a woman directing in 1913 | The first critique is dailies: the room watches your footage cold and silent. The film you shot is not the film you planned. That difference is your material now, not your failure. | What is in your footage that you did not put there? | read: Sidney Lumet, *Making Movies* (buy or borrow) · watch: *The Cutting Edge: The Magic of Movie Editing*, 2004 (link out) · ask: a photographer: "Tell me about the picture you didn't know you took." |
| 9 | Cut on the Blink | Every Frame a Painting, "How Does an Editor Think and Feel?" (official channel, embeddable) | Murch: the cut lands where the audience's attention finishes — the blink. Emotion first, story second, rhythm third. Then the room closes the tools: ninety minutes, unseen footage, a sixty-second scene, defended cut by cut. | Watch a scene tonight and say "now" where you would cut. Were you early or late? | read: Walter Murch, *In the Blink of an Eye* (buy or borrow) · watch: Vertov, *Man with a Movie Camera* again, this time for rhythm (Internet Archive) · ask: an editor: "What do you listen for?" |
| 10 | Knife | Alfred Hitchcock, *Blackmail*, 1929 (Internet Archive · public domain in the US since 2025) — the breakfast scene and the word "knife" | The picture shows; the sound tells you what it meant. In 1929 Hitchcock bent the soundtrack to a guilty woman's ear and the talkie learned to think. Record room tone. Record wild sound. Half the film is heard. | Close your eyes in a public place for sixty seconds and list the layers. | read: Michel Chion, *Audio-Vision* (buy or borrow) · watch: SoundWorks Collection, any sound-team profile (official channel) · ask: a location sound mixer: "What do directors do that ruins your day?" |
| 11 | Four Stems | Orson Welles and the Mercury Theatre on the Air, *The War of the Worlds*, 1938 (Internet Archive · public domain; confirm) — the first twelve minutes | Radio made a Martian invasion out of four stems: dialogue, effects, ambience, music. Each is a department in a big film and one person in yours. A film sounds finished before it looks finished. Do not steal music — write it, ask a friend, use Free Music Archive, or use silence. | Pick one scene you love and name the four stems in it, one at a time. | read: Walter Murch, "Dense Clarity — Clear Density" (transom.org, free) · hear: Ben Burtt on the sounds of *Star Wars* (Star Wars official channel; link out) · ask: a composer: "What do you need from a director that they never give you?" |
| 12 | Picture Lock | Edgar G. Ulmer, *Detour*, 1945 (Internet Archive · public domain) — the first ten minutes | Six days, one car, a rear-projected highway, sixty-eight minutes still watched eighty years on. Picture lock means the pictures stop changing so sound and color can finish. A finished small film beats an unfinished big one. The second critique: the rough cut, start to finish, nobody pauses. | If you had to lose a minute tonight, which minute? | read: Roger Ebert, "Detour" (Great Movies; rogerebert.com, free) · watch: Rudolph Maté, *D.O.A.*, 1949 (Internet Archive · public domain) · ask: someone who shipped a short: "What would you cut now?" |
| 13 | The Last Lie | Hand-colored and stencil-colored frames from early Pathé films, c. 1905–1910 (Wikimedia Commons · public domain) | Color was painted onto film by hand before it could be shot; the grade is the last lie you tell and the first the audience believes. Titles are typography. Delivery is a specification — aspect, frame rate, loudness — and the master lives in two places. | Notice the color of one scene tonight. Who chose it, and what for? | read: Alexis Van Hurkman, *Color Correction Handbook* (buy or borrow) · watch: Steve Yedlin, "Display Prep Demo" (yedlin.net, free) · ask: a colorist: "What do directors ask for that they don't mean?" |
| 14 | Say It in One Sentence | Poster for the Cinématographe Lumière, 1896 (Wikimedia Commons · public domain) — a crowd laughing at a gardener getting sprayed | The first film poster promised an audience a feeling, not a plot. The director's statement: 150 words — what it is, who it is for, why now — written in the room by hand. The logline: one sentence that makes someone want to sit in the dark. The screening is a public act; you invite the public. | Say your film in one sentence to someone who has not seen it. Watch their face, not their words. | read: François Truffaut, "A Certain Tendency of the French Cinema" (1954; in translation — library) · watch: Ira Glass on storytelling, 2009 (link out) · ask: a festival programmer: "What makes you stop a film at thirty seconds?" |
| 15 | The Room Is the Last Edit | La Paloma Theatre, Encinitas, opened 1928 (Wikimedia Commons, photograph · CC BY-SA; confirm file) — where surf films premiere | You sit in the dark with strangers and feel them breathe.
