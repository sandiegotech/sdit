# Arc — ENGR 320 Embedded Systems & Sensors

## The course in one paragraph

At the start you can write a program that runs on a laptop and blink a light on a microcontroller. At the end you can build a computer that lives inside a sealed thing, reads the world through sensors you have calibrated, keeps time, budgets its power, survives being dropped in the Pacific, and comes back with data you can set beside a hundred-year record and defend. The catalog register is embedded computing — microcontrollers, firmware, sensors, signal acquisition. The teaching frame is firmware, ocean sensors, instruments that survive saltwater. The ocean is the examiner in this course. It does not grade on a curve.

## The made thing

A working instrument deployed in the field, gathering real data in a hostile environment. Concretely: a microcontroller, at least two sensors (the default is water temperature and pressure, which together give a tide and temperature record), a clock, storage, a power source good for at least forty-eight hours, and an enclosure — sealed, built or adapted by you — deployed in saltwater: off Scripps Pier under the Shore Stations program's eye, on a mooring in Mission Bay, in a tide pool at Cabrillo, or, for the learner alone, in a bucket of seawater on a balcony for two days. "Finished" means the instrument went in, stayed in for the planned time, came out, and the data it carried has been plotted against a reference — the NOAA La Jolla tide gauge (station 9410230) and the Scripps Pier daily temperature — with the differences explained, and a deployment log and an instrument autopsy written. It is exhibited on Day 15: the instrument, opened; the data beside the reference; the log; the list of what leaked, what drifted, and what lied.

Day 1 states the premise. Day 2 gets the microcontroller blinking. Day 5 is a bench critique with two sensors logging to a file. Day 10 is the wet test — twenty-four hours sealed in a tank of seawater. Day 12 is deployment. Day 13 is retrieval and the dirty data. Day 15 is the exhibition.

## The unassisted examination

Day 11, tools closed, the week before deployment. You are given a raw voltage trace from a sensor, two pages of its datasheet, and a timestamped log with faults in it. In ninety minutes, by hand, you convert the trace to engineering units using the datasheet's curve, identify the noise, the dropout, and the drift, say what each one means about the hardware, and sketch the circuit — power, sensor, microcontroller, storage — that would have produced that log. Why this: the dashboard is downstream of everything. A graduate who cannot read the trace without the dashboard does not know whether the dashboard is lying, and in the field there is no dashboard.

## The studio

**The brief.** Build an instrument that measures something real in saltwater for at least forty-eight hours and brings the data back. The default instrument is a tide-and-temperature logger because the references exist and the comparison is unforgiving. Alternatives the studio accepts: a wave-motion logger (accelerometer on a float), a light-and-turbidity logger, a hydrophone. The instrument must have: at least two sensors you calibrated yourself against a known, a clock you set and checked, a power budget written before the battery is chosen, a watchdog, a sealed enclosure with every penetration listed, a retrieval plan, and a reference it will be compared against.

**Technique days.** Day 2 (the microcontroller and firmware without a net), Day 3 (what a sensor returns, and calibration), Day 4 (sealing against pressure and salt), Day 6 (timekeeping), Day 7 (power budgets and sleep), Day 8 (telemetry and spectrum), Day 9 (robust firmware: watchdogs, brownouts, recovery), Day 13 (retrieval and honest data).

**Critique days.** Day 5 (bench critique: raw data shown, nothing cleaned), Day 10 (the wet test: the tank is opened in front of the room), Day 14 (the data critique: everyone's traces beside the reference). The critique rule: the raw trace is shown first, always, before any plot that has been cleaned.

**Milestone days.** Day 2 (blinking and talking over serial), Day 5 (two sensors logging on the bench), Day 10 (twenty-four hours sealed and submerged), Day 12 (deployed), Day 13 (retrieved), Day 15 (exhibited).

**How the AI tools are used, and then closed.** The tools are open for reading datasheets, drafting drivers, decoding register maps, writing the logging and plotting code, and cleaning data. Every driver they draft is tested against the physical sensor before it is trusted; every cleaning step they propose is stated in the plot's caption; and on Day 11 you read a trace and sketch a circuit by hand. Command of intelligence here means directing the tools at the datasheet and verifying them against the voltmeter.

**What happens off the page.** The studio is a room with tools and a master in it — oscilloscopes, a bench supply, a soldering station, a pressure test chamber or a deep bucket, a tank of seawater, spare O-rings, and someone who has built instruments for Scripps or the Navy and lost one to the sea — and the page is the brief. In the room there is a deployment permission process (Scripps for the pier, the Port and the Coast Guard for the bay), a safety briefing for anyone on the water, and a shelf of flooded housings. A learner alone online does the smallest real version: a hobby microcontroller, a waterproof temperature probe and a pressure sensor, a lunchbox and silicone, a bucket of seawater on a balcony for forty-eight hours, and the NOAA tide prediction for the nearest station as a reference. That is a real instrument in a real hostile environment. The salt does not know it is a bucket.
