# Arc — HUM 201 The Enlightenment

## The course in one paragraph

At the start, the learner treats rights, markets, contracts, and constitutions as weather: things that are simply there. At the end, they can name the argument each one rests on, say who made it and what it was answering, and judge whether it still holds in a case they are living through this week. The catalog register is the political and economic philosophy of the Enlightenment: Hobbes, Locke, Smith, the American founders. The teaching frame reads those arguments as live questions, not museum pieces, with every text in the company of a film and an album that are still arguing the same point. The learner leaves able to find the design assumption inside an institution, which is the skill Madison had and most people who build systems today do not.

## The made thing

A made response to one live question, in one of four forms: an essay (1,200 to 2,000 words), a short film (under six minutes), a recorded piece, or a designed object with a one-page note. This semester's extra constraint comes from the course: the response must be addressed to someone who disagrees, the way *Common Sense* and the *Federalist* were. The learner names the reader they are trying to move on the first page. "Finished" means the thing exists, can be experienced without the maker present, names its text and its reader, and takes a position a reasonable person could reject. It is defended in seminar the week after Day 15, before peers who have been asked to take the other side: three minutes to present, seven of questions. It enters the portfolio and is read against Judgment, Articulation, and Breadth of Mind at the end-of-year gate.

Day 6 introduces it: standing in the Dust Bowl, where Locke's theory of property met people who had done all the labor and owned nothing, the course tells the learner to pick a live question they already argue about. Day 8 scopes it: the question, the form, and the reader, in three sentences. Day 11 starts it: Jefferson's rough draft, with its crossings-out, is the model, and a bad first draft is due within the week. Day 15 sets the defense: the founders built a system meant to be amended, and the made thing is argued as a proposed patch.

## The unassisted examination

In the week of Day 9, thirty minutes, tools closed: Adam Smith wrote the pin factory and, in the same book, the passage on what the pin factory does to the man who works in it. State both arguments accurately, then say which one the degree you are taking is designed to answer. Smith is the right text for this because he is the author most often quoted by people who have read one sentence of him, and the degree needs to see that the learner can carry a whole argument, including the part that cuts against the famous half. The question also asks the learner to turn the text on the institution itself, which is the kind of judgment the tools cannot supply. The Day 15 defense is also unaided.

## Rights and sources

- No Lectio edition covers this course's texts. All primary texts are PD on Project Gutenberg (#3207, #7370, #3300, #58559, #147, #1404) or in the National Archives (the Declaration, the Constitution, Founders Online). Passages may be quoted at length; on-page excerpts should stay under 300 words.
- Day 8: *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* is on Gutenberg inside *The Essays of Adam Smith* (#58559); confirm the volume includes the *Theory* in full before linking, otherwise use the Internet Archive scan of the 1790 sixth edition.
- Day 2 image: Wright's portrait of Hobbes is PD and the Commons file exists. Day 11 image: the Jefferson rough draft is a Library of Congress item and PD; the Commons file page needs confirming. Fallback is the Dunlap broadside, also PD.
- Day 3 and Day 13 on-page audio: official Columbia/Legacy uploads of "Folsom Prison Blues (Live)" and "So What" are likely embeddable from the rights holder, but unconfirmed. If not, the on-page thing is the text passage and the audio is a link.
- Day 14: the *12 Angry Men* theatrical trailer on the Internet Archive is marked public domain (trailers of that era were often not separately registered). Confirm before embedding; the film itself is in copyright and is a door.
- *Dust Bowl Ballads* (1940): the recordings are in copyright in the United States until 2041 under the Music Modernization Act; door only. The Library of Congress holds Guthrie's 1940 Lomax recordings, but their rights status is mixed; do not embed.
- *Seven Samurai*, *There Will Be Blood*, *Modern Times*, *12 Angry Men*, and all four albums are in copyright and are doors, named and linked to where they can be watched or bought.
- No day repeats a thing to experience used by a published day. Lewis Hine (Day 4) and Dorothea Lange (Day 6) are both PD federal photographs; HUM 202 uses a different industrial image (Menzel) to avoid leaning on Hine twice across the thread.

## Open questions for Brandon

1. *Kind of Blue* as the Founders' album: the argument is that a session with a few modal sketches and no charts is a constitution for six people, and it was recorded two years after *12 Angry Men*. The obvious alternative is *Hamilton*, which passes the recognition test and fails the founder's test, I think. Your call.
2. *Seven Samurai* is a Japanese film for a unit on an English philosopher, chosen because it is the cleanest dramatization of the contract as a transaction. If you want the Hobbes unit to stay in English, *Lord of the Flies* (1963) is the plain alternative.
3. The Founders unit runs six days (10 to 15), heavier than the others, because the degree is accredited in the United States and because the made thing this semester is an argument addressed to a disagreeing reader, which the *Federalist* models. If you would rather weight the four units evenly, Day 14 can fold into Day 13 and Smith gets a fourth day on Book 5's case for public education.
