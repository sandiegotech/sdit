# HUM 201 — The Enlightenment

**The Canon** · 2 units · Semester 3 · Required core — everyone takes it

## Overview

Rights, markets, contracts, constitutions: the operating system the modern world still runs on was written by a handful of people between 1651 and 1788, and most of us treat it as weather. This course reads the source. Hobbes on why you obey. Locke on why what you make is yours. Smith on why ten people doing one step each make more than one person doing all ten, and what that does to the ten. Madison on how to build a government for people who will not behave. Fifteen weeks inside the arguments that built the modern world, read as live questions, not museum pieces.

None of it is read alone. This is the Double Feature Method: every unit pairs **the text + a film + an album**, discussed as one conversation. *Leviathan* beside *Seven Samurai* and *At Folsom Prison*; Locke beside *There Will Be Blood*; *The Federalist* beside *12 Angry Men* and *Kind of Blue*. The point is not to admire the Enlightenment. It is to find the design assumption inside every institution you touch, which is the skill Madison had and most people who build systems today do not.

One day a week, about ten minutes a sitting, plus the reading that pulls you in. The course ends the way every SDIT course ends: in a made thing, defended out loud, this time to people who have been asked to disagree with you.

## The Two Registers

- **Catalog register:** Political and economic philosophy of the Enlightenment — Hobbes, Locke, Smith, the American founders. Close reading, discussion, and critical writing.
- **Teaching frame:** The arguments that built the modern world, read as live questions — not museum pieces. The text, a film, and an album, discussed as one conversation.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course you can:

1. Read an Enlightenment primary text closely and accurately reconstruct the argument it makes and the argument it was answering. → *Breadth of Mind* · *Critical thinking*
2. Find the design assumption inside an institution — a contract, a market, a constitution — and judge whether it still holds in a case you are living through. → *Judgment* · *Critical thinking*
3. Trace one idea across a text, a film, and an album, and defend the connection with evidence from all three. → *Judgment* · *Critical thinking*
4. Write an argument addressed to someone who disagrees, in plain English a stranger could follow, and revise it until it survives line-editing. → *Articulation* · *Written communication*
5. Produce a made response to a live question and defend it in seminar against peers taking the other side. → *Workmanship · Articulation* · *Oral communication*

*Each outcome maps to the five institutional outcomes (Judgment, Command of Intelligence, Workmanship, Articulation, Breadth of Mind) — the assessment spine of the degree and its accreditation evidence file.*

## The Made Thing

A made response to one live question — an essay, a short film, a recorded piece, or a designed object — addressed to someone who disagrees, the way *Common Sense* and *The Federalist* were. You name your reader on the first page and take a position a reasonable person could reject. It is defended in seminar before peers who have been asked to argue the other side, enters your portfolio, and is reviewed at the annual gate.

## How You're Assessed

- **Short written responses**, line-edited and revised — the work is the writing, not a quiz about the reading.
- **The made response**, defended out loud in seminar at the end of the course.
- **Periodic unassisted writing.** AI tools are open for drafting and study; the institution certifies the human, so part of your writing happens with the tools closed — including a timed response to Adam Smith's two books near the middle of the course.
- Everything lands in your **portfolio**, reviewed against the five outcomes at the end-of-year gate.
