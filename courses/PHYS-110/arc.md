# Arc — PHYS 110 Mechanics

## The course in one paragraph

At the start, the learner's body knows how to drop in, paddle, and lean through a corner, and cannot say what is pushing and what is slowing. At the end, the learner can watch any real maneuver, draw every force on it with a direction and a size, predict where it will go with energy and momentum, check the prediction by measurement, and say honestly how far the measurement can be trusted. That is the catalog register, classical mechanics: kinematics, forces, energy, momentum, rotation, with laboratory. The teaching frame is the ocean and the board — forces on a rail, pumping a transition, bottom turns, lean angle — with the lab on the sand and in the water. Force is a bottom turn before it is a vector; every law is met as something the knees already negotiate, then named, then measured.

## The made thing

A force analysis of a real maneuver, measured, diagrammed, defended. The learner picks one maneuver they or someone they can film actually does — a bottom turn, a drop-in and pump through a transition, a motorcycle lean through a corner, a paddle-out through whitewater, a kickflip — and records it: phone video frame-stepped against a known length, a free accelerometer app in the pocket, a stopwatch and a tape. Then: a free-body diagram at three instants, with every force named and sized from the data; the energy budget of the maneuver (where it came from, where it went); the momentum or angular momentum change where one occurs; one prediction made from the laws and checked against the recording; the uncertainty of each measured number stated and its source named. "Finished" means a lab notebook another student could reproduce the measurement from, one diagrammed figure, and a three-minute defense in which the learner is asked to change one assumption and say what moves. It is defended on Day 15 and enters the portfolio.

Day 3 introduces it (choose a maneuver you can film). Day 7 scopes it (film it this week against a measured length; the notebook opens). Day 11 starts the analysis (diagram the three instants, size the forces). Day 14 is the uncertainty audit. Day 15 is the defense.

## The unassisted examination

Two sittings, tools closed. After Day 9: a free-body diagram drawn from a photograph the learner has not seen, Newton's second law applied to one- and two-dimensional motion including friction and circular motion, and an energy-conservation prediction. After Day 14: a momentum problem, a torque and angular-momentum problem, and the reading of a measurement with its uncertainty. The diagram is examined unaided because drawing the forces is the whole skill; the rest follows from it. The uncertainty question is examined unaided because a machine will give any number of decimal places and only a person can say how many are real.

| Concept (catalog register) | Physical thing it arrives through, before notation | Where in San Diego |
|---|---|---|
| Kinematics: position, velocity, acceleration | Dropping a stone from the pier and counting; a frame-stepped drop-in; the odd-number pattern of a ball on a plank | Scripps Pier (dropped object, not from the deck — the sand below); Washington Street skatepark |
| Free fall and gravity | Everything falls at the same rate: a hammer and a feather; a wetsuit and a leash dropped together | Any beach stair |
| Vectors | A sail pulls one way and the keel another and the boat goes a third way; a bottom turn pushes down and in and the board goes up and out | Sailing on Mission Bay; Black's Beach |
| Newton's first law, inertia | Coasting a longboard on flat ground: nothing pushes, it keeps going; it is friction that stops it | Mission Beach boardwalk |
| Newton's second law | The same push moves a shortboard and a longboard differently; a loaded board takes more to turn | La Jolla Shores |
| Newton's third law | Paddling: push water back, go forward; the pier pilings push the water back as hard as it pushes them | Scripps Pier pilings on a swell |
| Friction | Wax on a deck, grip tape on a board, a slide on wet concrete; the rail that holds and the rail that slides | Any skatepark after rain; a waxed board |
| Projectiles | An air off the lip; a thrown ball on the sand; the arc is a parabola because gravity only pulls down | Ocean Beach; the lip at Sunset Cliffs |
| Circular motion and centripetal force | A carve in a bowl; a bottom turn; the lean angle of a motorcycle through a corner is the ratio of two forces | Washington Street bowl; the curves on Catalina Boulevard, Point Loma |
| Work and energy | Pumping a transition: the legs add energy on the way down; the drop-in height becomes speed at the bottom | Washington Street skatepark |
| Conservation of energy | A pendulum returns to its height and no higher; a wave's height becomes its speed as it stands up | A swing at Kellogg Park; Black's |
| Momentum, impulse, collisions | Two boards bumping in the lineup; landing soft-kneed to stretch the stop out in time; a Newton's cradle | Any lineup; any landing |
| Center of mass | Crouching to make it through the turn; a falling slinky whose bottom waits; a surfer's stance | La Jolla Shores lineup |
| Torque and rotational inertia | A kickflip spins about the board's long axis because that axis has the least inertia; a wheel's mass at the rim | Any skateboard, held and spun |
| Angular momentum | Arms out slows a spin, arms in speeds it; a cutback thrown with the shoulders; a spinning wheel resists tilting | Any bowl; a bike wheel held by the axle |
| Laboratory: measurement and uncertainty | A tide predicted and a tide observed; a wave period timed ten times and never the same | NOAA La Jolla tide station 9410230; Scripps Pier |
