Day 02 — The Hammer and the Feather

Ten minutes on the page. One astronaut, two objects, one experiment you repeat in your kitchen.

Look

He did it on live television because a lot of people did not believe it. Galileo had argued it three and a half centuries earlier, from a tower and a thought experiment, and most people still carry the older idea in their bodies: heavy things fall faster. On the Moon there is no air, and the feather drops like a stone.

One idea

Everything falls the same way. Air is what makes it look otherwise.

Drop a hammer and a feather in your kitchen and the feather loses, but not because it is light. It loses because air pushes back on it far more, for its weight, than air pushes back on the hammer. Take the air away and the race is a tie. Crumple a sheet of paper and drop it beside a flat one: same paper, same weight, and the crumpled one wins. You changed nothing about gravity. You changed how much air the thing has to shove aside.

And falling is not a speed. It is a steady gaining of speed. One second after you let go, anything is moving about ten meters a second faster than it was. Two seconds, twenty. The catalog calls this course mechanics, and the first three words of mechanics are the three things you just watched: where the hammer is, how fast it is going, and how fast that is changing. Position, velocity, acceleration. On the Moon the third one is smaller, about a sixth of ours, which is why the fall on the film looks lazy, and why it looks the same for both objects.

Yesterday you asked what pushes a moving thing and what slows it. Today the answer for falling is: gravity pushes, air slows, and when you remove the second, the first treats everything alike.

Carry the question

For the rest of today, carry this one:

Drop two different things together today and watch which lands first. Then ask why.

Answer in a line or a page, or not at all. What you write is kept in your Notebook.

That’s it. Day 2 is done.

If you’re still curious

Three doors. Open one, or none — they’ll be here tomorrow too.

Read the real thing. Galileo, Dialogues Concerning Two New Sciences (1638; Crew and de Salvio’s 1914 translation, Internet Archive). The First Day, the first few pages on falling bodies, where he argues the tie before anyone could test it.

Watch the misconception get caught. Veritasium, “Misconceptions About Falling Objects”. Three minutes of people on a street guessing wrong, confidently, and one of them changing his mind on camera.

Ask someone. Ask a lifeguard: “How long does a jump from the pier take, and how do you know?” Listen for whether the answer is a number or a feeling. By Day 5 you will be able to give them both.
