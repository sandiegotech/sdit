# Arc — CS 220 Machine Learning I

## The course in one paragraph

At the start, the model that finishes your sentences is a black box you talk to. At the end, you have built one — small, from scratch, in plain code — trained it on data you gathered yourself, measured exactly where it fails, and written that down honestly. The catalog register is the foundations of machine learning: models, training, evaluation, limits. The teaching frame is Teaching Machines: what a model is, how it trains, what it can and cannot know. Every concept arrives physically first — a ruler through a scatter of points, a hot-and-cold game down a hillside, a horse that learned the wrong thing — then as a measurement on your own data, and only then as the notation from MATH 210 and MATH 220. You leave able to say what any model is doing, to build a small one, and to read an evaluation the way a sailor reads a chart: for where the rocks are.

## The made thing

A model trained from scratch on data you gathered — with an honest evaluation of where it fails. "From scratch" means the gradient is yours: plain Python with array arithmetic (numpy), no training library, so every number the model learns passed through code you can point to. "Data you gathered" means you collected and labelled it, starting Day 2. Two default tracks: **the daily log** — one labelled observation a day for the course (your own call on surf, mood, focus, sleep, or commute against public measurements: CDIP buoys, NOAA weather, your own sleep tracker), which teaches small data honestly; or **the afternoon set** — a few hundred photographs or short texts of your own, labelled in two or three classes in one sitting (your two dogs, your handwriting versus a friend's, messages from two group chats). "Finished" means four things ship: the data, with a one-paragraph account of how it was gathered and what is missing; the model, with its training code and loss curve; the evaluation on held-out data — a confusion matrix, and every wrong prediction examined one by one; and the failure report: what the model cannot know, who it fails, and what would have to be true of the data for it to be trusted. It is defended by demonstration — the model predicts on a new example the examiner brings — and by questioning on the report.

Day 2 starts the data. Day 5 fits the first model to it. Day 10 trains the second with a held-out set. Day 12 examines the failures. Day 15 ships. The learner knows what they are making by Day 2 and has begun that day.

## The unassisted examination

Twice, tools closed, on paper. **Day 8:** given three data points and a line, compute the squared-error loss, compute the gradient with respect to the slope, and take one step; then explain in three sentences why a model must be tested on data it did not see. **Day 14:** read a confusion matrix printed on the page and say which error matters and why a model can be 99 percent accurate and useless; sketch a two-layer network and name where the gradient flows; and say, in a paragraph, what the model you built cannot know. These are examined because they are the two halves of the course's honesty: a person who cannot take one gradient step by hand has not understood training, and a person who cannot read a confusion matrix cannot evaluate anything the assistant tells them. Pass or revise; repeated at the annual gate.

## The build sequence

**Day 5:** a one-variable linear model, fit by gradient descent you wrote (twenty lines, no library), on the first two weeks of your data. A loss curve going down. One step checked by hand against the code.

**Day 10:** a small neural network — one hidden layer, numpy, your own backward pass — trained on your data with a held-out test set, producing a confusion matrix. Every gradient checked numerically against a finite difference at least once. The assistant wrote some of the backward pass; you found the sign it got wrong.

**Day 15:** the data, the model, the evaluation, and the failure report, defended on a new example.

**How the AI tools are used:** open from Day 2. The assistant is excellent at writing training code and worse at knowing whether it is correct; the course's verification method is the finite-difference check — perturb a weight by a tiny amount, see whether the loss moves the way the gradient said — and the held-out set. Days 6, 9, and 13 are explicitly about what the assistant is: a model of the same kind you are building, trained on text, which means its confident explanation of your model may be exactly as wrong as your model's confident prediction. **How the human is examined unaided:** Days 8 and 14, as above, plus the standing rule: any number in your training loop may be asked about, and "the assistant wrote it" is not an answer.

## Open questions for Brandon

1. **The daily log as default.** Fifteen weeks of one observation a day gives about a hundred examples — honest, personal, and thin. It teaches small-data humility but makes Day 10's network nearly pointless on that track. Should the daily log be the default with the afternoon set as the alternative, or the reverse?
2. **numpy or nothing.** "From scratch" here means plain Python with numpy. The alternative is pure Python lists, slower but with nothing hidden. Which is the Institute's line?
3. **CDIP again.** CS 210 proposes CDIP buoy data as its default dataset. If both courses lean on it, a learner's CS 220 daily log against the buoys becomes a two-course project. Is that a feature or a rut?
