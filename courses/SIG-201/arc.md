# Arc — SIG 201 How Images Move

## The course in one paragraph

At the start you scroll. At the end you can look at any moving image — a 1895 train, a 1936 photograph, a six-second loop — and say what the frame leaves out, where the first cut lands and what it does, who it was made for, what claim it makes, and whether it will travel, with reasons you can be held to. The catalog register is visual media systems: cinema, short-form ecosystems, documentary, attention economics. The teaching frame is the question why some images travel and others die — craft, platforms, and the economics of attention. You learn it on public-domain film and photographs that moved the world, on the skate videos that were cut in San Diego on two VCRs, on the Hall H line, and on the Navy films this town made about itself for ninety years. Then you make a short piece, release it into a real feed, and write honestly about what the feed did with it.

## The made thing

A short visual piece — thirty seconds to three minutes, shot and cut by the learner — released into a real feed under the learner's own account (YouTube, Instagram, TikTok, Vimeo, or the Institute's channel; the learner names the feed and says why), on a stated date, with a written account of how it moved.

"Finished" means three things. The piece is released where strangers can see it. The account (600 to 900 words) is written after fourteen days with the numbers in hand — views, average view duration or completion, shares and saves, comments, where viewers came from — and separates what moved from what was merely counted. And the learner's Day 11 prediction about the piece sits beside the result, right or wrong.

Day 1 announces it. Day 4 scopes it: documentary or made, which real place or thing, which feed. Day 8 starts it: the first shoot, with the skate-video grammar of Day 8 as a first vocabulary. Day 13 is the model for the ninety-second documentary. Day 15 is release day and the defense: the piece shown once, the account read aloud in five minutes, the prediction beside the numbers. The Institute mirrors pieces on its channel with the learner's permission. The thing enters the portfolio and is read at the end-of-year gate, with the fourteen-day numbers.

## The unassisted examination

Week 11, after Day 11, twenty-five minutes, tools closed. The learner watches one unfamiliar short piece (under two minutes) twice and writes: what the frame excludes; where the first cut lands and what it does; who the piece was made for; what claim it makes; and a prediction, with reasons, of whether it will travel in a named feed. The predictions are sealed and checked on Day 14 against the piece's real numbers. The course's skill is judging images; a prediction with reasons is judgment in a form that can be scored.

## The signal

The real artifact shipped is the short piece in a real feed the learner does not control. The audience is whoever that feed shows it to, plus the cohort and the Institute's channel. Movement is measured at fourteen days, on the platform's own analytics: views; average view duration and the fraction who finished; shares, saves, and comments; traffic sources (feed, search, external); and one qualitative mark — the best comment, or the most telling silence. The written account is the second artifact and the one the gate reads first: it must distinguish reach (how many saw it) from effect (what anyone did), and it must say what the learner would change. Small honest numbers are the standard; a piece that reached two hundred people and made three of them write back has moved.
