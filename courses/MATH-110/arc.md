# Arc — MATH 110 Calculus I

## The course in one paragraph

At the start, the learner can feel a drop-in get steep and a swell fade, and cannot say what either of those is. At the end, the learner can take any moving thing — a ride, a fall, a tide, a glider — record it, and say with the derivative exactly how fast it is changing at any instant, where its rate is greatest, where it turns, and what the best choice is when something must be made largest or smallest. That is the catalog register, differential calculus: limits, derivatives, optimization, with applications. The teaching frame is the mathematics of motion — ramp transitions, racing lines, swell decay — and the rule, held every day, is that nothing arrives as notation first. The learner sees it move, then names it, then computes with it, then is examined on the computation with the tools closed.

## The made thing

A motion study. The learner picks one real moving thing in San Diego — a skater through the Washington Street bowl, a wave fading toward Scripps Pier, a ball thrown on the sand, a glider off Torrey Pines, a song's loudness envelope — and records it (a phone video frame-stepped, a free accelerometer app, a tide gauge, a stopwatch). Then: position against time plotted from the data; velocity and acceleration found from it, numerically and by a fitted function; the instant of greatest speed, the turning point, the inflection, each named on the data and on the graph; one optimization or related-rates question asked of the same motion and answered. "Finished" means a one-page figure with the data and the mathematics side by side, and a two-minute spoken defense of the setup, not only the answer. It is defended in seminar on Day 15 and enters the portfolio.

Day 6 introduces it (record one motion this week). Day 10 scopes it (choose the one you will keep, and the one question you will ask of it). Day 13 starts the modeling (fit, differentiate, mark the instants). Day 15 is the defense of a first version; the finished study is due at the semester's end.

## The unassisted examination

Two sittings, tools closed. After Day 9: limits by zooming, the derivative from the definition for one simple function, and the rules — power, sum, product, chain, trig, exponential — applied by hand to functions the learner has not seen. After Day 14: one optimization and one related-rates problem set up from words, solved, and checked at the endpoints. The rules are examined unaided because they have to live in the hand before the hand can check what a machine gives back; the word problems are examined unaided because setup is the judgment the course is for.
