HUM 102 — Rome to the Renaissance

The Canon · 2 units · Semester 2 · Required core — everyone takes it

Overview

Thirteen centuries separate the last Roman emperor worth reading from the workshop where Leonardo learned to paint, and most people think of them as a gap. This course reads them as one line. An emperor writing notes to himself in a war camp. A bishop inventing the inner autobiography. An exile mapping the whole moral universe in verse. A tortured diplomat writing the first honest book about power. An apprentice who never separated drawing from engineering. Fifteen weeks inside Aurelius, Augustine, Dante, Machiavelli, and da Vinci’s notebooks, and none of it read alone. This is the Double Feature Method: every unit pairs the text + a film + an album, discussed as one conversation. Meditations beside Dogtown and Z-Boys; Confessions beside A Love Supreme; The Prince beside The Godfather.

The course ends in the bottega, where art and engineering were one job, because that room is the model for this whole institution. Da Vinci’s notebooks are the founding documents of the premise that technology, art, craft, science, and philosophy are one discipline. You read them here to see what it looks like when a person refuses to divide them.

One day a week, about ten minutes a sitting, plus the reading that pulls you in. Where a Lectio edition exists, you read in it. The course ends the way every SDIT course ends: in a made thing, defended out loud.

The Two Registers

  • Catalog register: Roman, medieval, and Renaissance texts — Aurelius, Augustine, Dante, Machiavelli, da Vinci’s notebooks. Close reading, discussion, and critical writing.
  • Teaching frame: The Double Feature — Aurelius + Dogtown and Z-Boys; da Vinci’s notebooks as the founding model of the artist-engineer. The text, a film, and an album, discussed as one conversation.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course you can:

  1. Read a Roman, medieval, or Renaissance primary text closely and accurately reconstruct what it asks of its reader. → Breadth of Mind
  2. Trace one idea across a text, a film, and an album separated by centuries, and defend the connection with evidence from all three. → Judgment
  3. Hold an argument you dislike — Machiavelli’s, for instance — accurately enough that its author would sign it, then answer it in your own voice. → Judgment · Articulation
  4. Write short critical responses that make a claim, support it from the text, and survive line-editing. → Articulation
  5. Produce a made response under a named constraint, and defend it in seminar. → Workmanship · Articulation

Each outcome maps to the five institutional outcomes (Judgment, Command of Intelligence, Workmanship, Articulation, Breadth of Mind) — the assessment spine of the degree and its accreditation evidence file.

The Made Thing

A made response to this material — an essay, a short film, a recorded piece, or a designed object — made with what is at hand. Marcus wrote in a tent; the Z-Boys skated drained pools; Leonardo drew water in a notebook. You name your constraint on the first page and work inside it. The response is defended in seminar at the end of the course, enters your portfolio, and is reviewed at the annual gate. Because Semester 2 ends in the public exhibition, it may also be shown there.

How You’re Assessed

  • Short written responses, line-edited and revised — the work is the writing, not a quiz about the reading.
  • The made response, defended out loud in seminar at the end of the course.
  • Periodic unassisted writing. AI tools are open for drafting and study; the institution certifies the human, so part of your writing happens with the tools closed — including a timed response to The Prince in the final weeks.
  • Everything lands in your portfolio, reviewed against the five outcomes at the end-of-year gate.
