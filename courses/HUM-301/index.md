# HUM 301 — The Age of Intelligence

**The Canon** · 2 units · Semester 5 · Required core — everyone takes it

## Overview

In 1950 Alan Turing asked whether a machine could think, decided the question was unanswerable as asked, and replaced it with a game. Two years earlier Norbert Wiener had named a new science after the Greek word for steersman and warned what it would do to work. Both were late. Samuel Butler had machines evolving in 1872, E. M. Forster had the network and the screen in 1909, and Karel Čapek coined the word *robot* in 1920, in a play about labor. This course reads the founding papers of machine intelligence next to the fiction that got there first, and keeps score.

It is taught by the Double Feature Method, as every Canon course is: each unit pairs **the text + a film + an album**, discussed as one conversation. Turing beside *2001* and Kraftwerk; Wiener beside *Dr. Strangelove* and Brian Eno; Goethe's sorcerer's apprentice beside *Ex Machina* and a record Holly Herndon made with an AI she raised in her choir. The alignment problem turns out to be a poem from 1797. You will read it in the original form before you read it as a paper.

You are in your third year, and you have built models in CS 220. This is the course where you find out what the people who started the field actually said, and how much of it they got right. It ends, as every SDIT course ends, in a made thing: a forecast of your own, with a mechanism, defended against a room trying to prove it already wrong.

## The Two Registers

- **Catalog register:** Twentieth- and twenty-first-century writings on computation and intelligence — Turing, Wiener, the cybernetics and alignment corpus; science fiction as forecasting.
- **Teaching frame:** Reading the field's founding papers next to the fiction that predicted them — and grading both against the century that followed.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course you can:

1. Read a founding paper of the field — Turing (1950), Wiener (1948, 1950, 1960) — and restate its central claim, its assumptions, and the strongest objection it answers. → *Breadth of Mind*
2. Read a work of fiction as a forecast: name the claim, the mechanism, and what the record since has confirmed or refuted. → *Judgment*
3. State the alignment problem in plain language, trace it from Goethe and Wiener to the contemporary corpus, and recognize it in a system you have built. → *Command of Intelligence*
4. Produce a forecast of your own — a story, a short film, a recorded piece, or a designed object — with a one-page note stating its claim, mechanism, and falsifier, and defend it in seminar. → *Judgment · Command of Intelligence*

*Each outcome maps to the five institutional outcomes (Judgment, Command of Intelligence, Workmanship, Articulation, Breadth of Mind) — the assessment spine of the degree and its accreditation evidence file.*

## The Made Thing

A forecast: one piece — a short story, a short film, a recorded piece, or a designed object — set in a named year between 2040 and 2076, committing to one specific claim about machine intelligence and people. It travels with a one-page forecaster's note: the claim, the founding text it extends or contradicts, the mechanism, and what evidence by that year would prove it wrong. AI tools are open in the making, and the note says what the model made and what you judged. It is defended in seminar, where the room's one job is to argue the forecast is already wrong. It enters your portfolio and is reviewed at the annual gate.

## How You're Assessed

- **Short written responses**, line-edited and revised — a forecast graded, a paper restated, an objection answered.
- **The forecast and its note**, defended out loud in seminar at the end of the course.
- **One unassisted examination**, tools closed: Turing's argument in your own words, the objection he answered, and whether his forecast came true. A course about machines that imitate human speech ends with the human heard alone.
- Everything lands in your **portfolio**, reviewed against the five outcomes at the end-of-year gate.
