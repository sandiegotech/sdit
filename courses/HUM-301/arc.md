# Arc — HUM 301 The Age of Intelligence

## The course in one paragraph

At the end of this course you can pick up a founding paper of the field — Turing in 1950, Wiener in 1948 and 1950 — and say what it claims, what it assumed, and where the seventy years since have proved it right or wrong. You can read a piece of fiction as a forecast and grade it. You can state the alignment problem in plain words and trace it back to a poem Goethe wrote in 1797 about a broom that would not stop. The catalog register is twentieth- and twenty-first-century writings on computation and intelligence — Turing, Wiener, the cybernetics and alignment corpus; science fiction as forecasting. The teaching frame is reading the field's founding papers next to the fiction that predicted them, and keeping score.

## The made thing

**A Forecast.** One piece — a short story, a short film, a recorded piece, or a designed object — set in a named year between 2040 and 2076, that commits to one specific claim about machine intelligence and people. It travels with a one-page *forecaster's note*: the claim in one sentence; the founding text it extends or contradicts (Turing, Wiener, Čapek, Forster, Butler, Goethe); the mechanism that gets the world from here to there; and what evidence, by the named year, would prove it wrong. The model is Wells in 1914 and Szilard in 1933: a forecast with a mechanism, not a mood.

*Finished* means: the piece runs under ten minutes to read, watch, hear, or handle; the note fits on one page; the claim is falsifiable. AI tools are open in the making, and the note says what the model made and what the learner judged. It is defended in seminar, where the room's one job is to argue that the forecast is already wrong, and the learner's one job is to show the mechanism holds.

Day 1 introduces it (fiction as a forecasting method with a track record). Day 5 scopes it (the forecaster's note, modeled on Wells and Szilard). Day 9 is when each learner names their claim. Day 12 drafts the note. Day 13 workshops the pieces aloud. Day 14 examines the learner unaided. Day 15 is the defense. By Day 15 every learner has a forecast with a mechanism and has heard the room try to break it.

## The unassisted examination

Day 14, one sitting, forty-five minutes, tools closed. Three tasks on paper: restate Turing's central move in your own words (what question he replaced, and with what); state the strongest objection he answered and how; say whether his forecast about the year 2000 came true, with one reason. No notes, no model. A course about machines that imitate human speech has to end with the human heard speaking alone. If the learner cannot do this without a tool, nothing in the course was theirs.
