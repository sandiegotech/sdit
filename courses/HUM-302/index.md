# HUM 302 — Ethics & Statecraft

**The Canon** · 2 units · Semester 6 · Required core — everyone takes it

## Overview

What you build changes who has power. A bridge, a weapon, a drug, a platform, a model: each one moves power from someone to someone, and most builders never ask where. This course is the standing argument about what that obliges you to do. It borrows its vocabulary from the oldest serious thinking about force — just war theory's questions of cause, authority, proportion, discrimination, and last resort — and carries it from the battlefield to the bench, where you work.

It is argued through real cases, with the primary record open: the Szilard petition and the Franck Report, Feynman's appendix to the Challenger report, the engineer who found his own fatal mistake in a Manhattan skyscraper and picked up the phone, the software that flew a 737 into the ground. Each unit is a Double Feature — **the text + a film + an album** as one conversation: Aquinas beside *The Fog of War* and Marvin Gaye; the Los Alamos scientists beside *The Day After Trinity* and Dylan; Eisenhower's farewell beside *The Lives of Others* and Public Enemy. You take this course in the same semester as Field Term, from inside a real organization. The cases are not far away.

It ends in a made thing: a brief on one real case from the field you are entering, argued to a verdict and defended in seminar as a tribunal, with a classmate as counsel for the other side and an outside examiner asking the last question.

## The Two Registers

- **Catalog register:** Ethics of technology and power — just war theory, statecraft, the responsibilities of builders.
- **Teaching frame:** The builder's responsibility, argued through real cases from the primary record — reports, memos, testimony, code.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course you can:

1. Apply the vocabulary of just war theory — cause, authority, proportion, discrimination, last resort — to a built thing, and say where the analogy holds and where it breaks. → *Judgment · Breadth of Mind*
2. Read a primary record — a commission report, a memo, a petition, a speech — and reconstruct what the builders knew, when, and what they did with it. → *Judgment*
3. Hear a statesman's argument about power and say what it asks engineers to build, and for whom. → *Breadth of Mind*
4. Write a case brief in plain words — verdict, principle, the strongest counter-argument and its answer — and defend it out loud against someone who has read the same record. → *Articulation*
5. Write that brief on a case you have not seen, in one sitting, with the tools closed. → *Articulation · Judgment*

*Each outcome maps to the five institutional outcomes (Judgment, Command of Intelligence, Workmanship, Articulation, Breadth of Mind) — the assessment spine of the degree and its accreditation evidence file.*

## The Made Thing

A case brief on one real built thing from the field you are entering or from your Field Term placement: what was built, who it gave power to, what its builders knew and when, and what they owed — argued to a verdict, citing the primary record, anticipating the strongest argument for the other side, and free of euphemism. The form is an essay of about two thousand words, a short film, a recorded piece, or a designed object with a written brief. It is defended in seminar as a tribunal. It enters your portfolio and is reviewed at the annual gate.

## How You're Assessed

- **Short written responses**, line-edited and revised — the five words applied to a case, a record reconstructed, a speech read for what it asks of builders.
- **The brief**, defended as a tribunal at the end of the course, with counsel for the other side and an outside examiner.
- **One unassisted examination**, tools closed: a brief on a case you have not seen, written in forty-five minutes. A builder has to say what they think about what they built without a model drafting it.
- Everything lands in your **portfolio**, reviewed against the five outcomes at the end-of-year gate.
