# Arc — HUM 302 Ethics & Statecraft

## The course in one paragraph

At the end of this course you can take a real case — a weapon, a bridge, a drug, a platform, a model — and argue in writing and out loud what its builders owed, to whom, and whether they paid it. You do it with the vocabulary of just war theory (cause, authority, proportion, discrimination, last resort) carried from the battlefield to the bench. You can hear a statesman's speech and say what it asks engineers to build. The catalog register is ethics of technology and power — just war theory, statecraft, the responsibilities of builders. The teaching frame is the builder's responsibility, argued through real cases. This course runs in the same semester as Field Term, so most learners take it from inside a real organization; the cases are not far away.

## The made thing

**The Brief.** A case brief on one real built thing, chosen from the field the learner is entering or from the Field Term placement itself: what was built, who it gave power to, what its builders knew and when, and what they owed — argued to a verdict. The form is an essay of about two thousand words, a short film, a recorded piece, or a designed object with a written brief attached.

*Finished* means: it states a verdict; it cites the primary record (reports, memos, testimony, code, drawings), not the coverage; it anticipates the strongest argument for the other side and answers it; it contains no euphemism. It is defended in seminar as a tribunal. A classmate is assigned as counsel for the other side and has read the record. An outside examiner — a lawyer, an officer, or a Field Term mentor — asks the last question.

Day 1 introduces it (every built thing is a transfer of power). Day 4 scopes it (the five words, applied to the bench; the structure of a brief). The case is chosen by Day 8. The primary record is gathered by Day 11. Day 13 reads drafts aloud and strikes the euphemisms. Day 14 examines the learner unaided on a case they have not seen. Day 15 is the tribunal. By Day 15 every learner has argued a real case to a verdict against someone who read the same record.

## The unassisted examination

Day 14, one sitting, forty-five minutes, tools closed. The learner is handed a one-page summary of a real case they have not studied, with two short primary documents attached. They write the brief: verdict, the principle it rests on, the strongest counter-argument, and the answer to it. Articulation is this course's outcome. In a real room a builder has to say what they think about what they built without a model drafting it, and this is where that is observed.

## Open questions for Brandon

1. How close to home does the course go? Day 11's case could be named — General Atomics in Poway, the Navy's NIWC on Point Loma — or left for the learner to choose. Naming it is honest about where SDIT lives; it also puts a neighbor on trial.
2. Unit 2's film: *The Day After Trinity* (the builders in their own words, 1981) or *Oppenheimer* (2023, the one the cohort has seen)? The arc leads with the documentary and keeps the feature as a door.
3. Who is the outside examiner at the Day 15 tribunal — a lawyer, a serving or retired officer, or the learner's own Field Term mentor? The arc assumes a mentor; a lawyer would make it a sharper room.
