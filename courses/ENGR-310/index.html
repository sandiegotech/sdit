ENGR 310 — Mechanical Design & Fabrication

The Depths · 4 units · Semester 5 · Depth studio — Design & Engineering · the Crossing for others

Overview

From the sketch to the CAD model to the part in your hand. This studio goes the whole way, and its subject is the gap at the end: between what the drawing claims and what the part is. A dimension is a claim. A tolerance is how honest you are about it — too tight and you lie about your shop, too loose and you lie about the function. Hammurabi put the builder’s honesty on the same page as murder. Tolerance is a moral category, and in this studio it becomes a column of measured numbers beside a working mechanism.

The catalog says mechanical engineering design: CAD, materials, manufacturing processes, tolerance. The room says: pick a mechanism with at least three moving parts and one fit that matters — a skateboard truck, a fin and its box, a latch for a dive housing, a quick-release for a drone payload — and make it work. Each week on the page is one idea and one thing to experience: a two-thousand-year-old gear train, an airliner that failed from tiredness, a bridge that danced itself apart, a watch judged by five seconds. Each week in the shop you draw, cut, measure, and record.

This is a depth studio: a room with a mill, a lathe, a printer, a surface plate, and a machinist who has scrapped parts worth more than a semester. The page is the brief. The course ends in a made thing — the part, the drawing, and the numbers, on one table, with you answering for the gap between them.

The Two Registers

  • Catalog register: Mechanical engineering design — CAD, materials, manufacturing processes, tolerance.
  • Teaching frame: CAD to shop floor; tolerance is a moral category.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course you can:

  1. Produce an engineering drawing package — orthographic views, datums, dimensions, and tolerances with stated reasons — that a stranger could manufacture from without you in the room. → Workmanship
  2. Choose a material and a manufacturing process for a part from its load, its cycles, its environment, and the shop available, and say what each choice gives up. → Judgment
  3. Specify the fit that matters in a mechanism and hold it: shaft and hole, clearance, transition, or interference, measured and recorded. → Workmanship
  4. Fabricate a mechanism by at least two processes, inspect it against its drawing, and record every critical dimension honestly on a first-article sheet. → Workmanship
  5. Read a drawing and a part cold, judge pass or fail, and say what would happen in service — with the tools closed. → Judgment

Each outcome maps to the five institutional outcomes (Judgment, Command of Intelligence, Workmanship, Articulation, Breadth of Mind) — the assessment spine of the degree and its accreditation evidence file.

The Made Thing

A designed and fabricated mechanism that works — drawings, tolerances, and the physical thing. At least three moving parts and one fit that matters; a complete drawing package to a stated standard with every critical tolerance explained; the parts made by you by at least two processes; a first-article inspection sheet with every critical dimension measured in ink; and the assembled mechanism performing its function in front of the room for a hundred cycles. It is exhibited on the last day with the drawing and the numbers beside it, and it enters your portfolio for review at the annual gate.

How You’re Assessed

  • The drawing package, reviewed by the room as a shop would read it before anything is cut.
  • The mechanism, judged by whether it does the function stated in a number on Day 2, and by what wears, loosens, or cracks under a hundred cycles.
  • The inspection sheet, judged by whether every critical dimension is measured, recorded, and compared to the drawing without rounding.
  • Three studio critiques — Day 5 (the cardboard mock-up), Day 10 (the drawing review), Day 14 (the cycle test) — in which the room handles your thing and your drawing in silence first, then out loud.
  • An unassisted examination on Day 11, tools closed: you read a drawing you have not seen, measure the part made from it, judge it, and hand-sketch and dimension an object from the bench. The institution certifies the human who can speak the drawing’s language without software.
  • Everything lands in your portfolio, reviewed against the five outcomes at the end-of-year gate.
