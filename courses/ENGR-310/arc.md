# Arc — ENGR 310 Mechanical Design & Fabrication

## The course in one paragraph

At the start you can sketch a mechanism and model it in CAD. At the end you can draw it so a stranger can make it, state the tolerances that make it work and no tighter, choose the material and the process that can hold them, make the part, measure it, write down the numbers, and put the working thing on the table beside the drawing. The catalog register is mechanical engineering design — CAD, materials, manufacturing processes, tolerance. The teaching frame is CAD to shop floor, and the sentence the studio is built on: tolerance is a moral category. A dimension is a claim. A tolerance is how honest you are about it.

## The made thing

A designed and fabricated mechanism that works — drawings, tolerances, and the physical thing. Concretely: a mechanism with at least three moving parts and one fit that matters — a shaft in a bearing, a pin in a hinge, a latch that must seat, a thread that must hold. Defaults the studio offers, each from a Workshop material tradition: a skateboard truck (baseplate, hanger, kingpin, bushings, axle); a surfboard fin and the box it locks into; a latch for a dive-camera housing that must seal under load; a quick-release for a drone sensor payload. "Finished" means: a complete drawing package to a stated standard, every critical dimension toleranced and the reason for the tolerance written in a note; the parts made, by you, by at least two processes (printed and machined, or cast and machined); a first-article inspection sheet with every critical dimension measured and recorded; and the assembled mechanism performing its function in front of the room for at least a hundred cycles. It is exhibited on Day 15: the part, the drawing, and the numbers, side by side, with the learner answering for the gap between them.

Day 1 states the premise. Day 2 is the brief: you pick a mechanism and a function it must perform. Day 5 is a paper and cardboard mock-up that moves. Day 10 is the drawing review — nothing is cut until the drawing passes. Day 13 is first-article inspection. Day 14 is the cycle test. Day 15 is the exhibition.

## The unassisted examination

Day 11, tools closed. You are handed a drawing you have never seen and the part made from it, with calipers and a micrometer. In ninety minutes you say what the part is for, name the critical fit, measure the part, state whether it passes, and say what would happen in service if it does not. Then you hand-sketch three orthographic views of a small object placed on the bench, dimensioned, with one tolerance you can defend. No CAD, no assistant. Why this: the drawing is a language, and a graduate who can only speak it through software cannot read a shop's question back, cannot catch a model's error, and cannot be trusted to sign a drawing.

## The studio

**The brief.** Design and make a mechanism with at least three moving parts and one fit that matters, and prove it works by drawing, by measurement, and by use. The function comes first — "holds a 7-inch fin against a 40-pound side load and releases with one screw" — and everything else follows from it. The studio keeps a shelf of past mechanisms, broken and working, and you will handle them on Day 2.

**Technique days.** Day 3 (the drawing as a language), Day 4 (materials and fatigue), Day 6 (fits and datums), Day 7 (processes — subtractive, additive, formative, cutting), Day 8 (metrology), Day 9 (load paths and stress concentration), Day 12 (fasteners, finishes, and the salt air).

**Critique days.** Day 5 (mock-up critique: does it move the way you said?), Day 10 (drawing review: could a stranger make it?), Day 14 (cycle test: what wore, what loosened, what cracked?). A critique here is the room handling your thing and your drawing, in silence first, then out loud.

**Milestone days.** Day 2 (function stated, mechanism chosen), Day 5 (cardboard mock-up that moves), Day 10 (drawing package passes review), Day 13 (first article made and inspected), Day 15 (exhibited, working).

**How the AI tools are used, and then closed.** CAD assistants, generative design, and language models are open for concept generation, for working out stack-ups, for drafting notes and reading standards. Every number they produce is checked against a hand calculation before it goes on a drawing, and the drawing is yours — your name is in the title block, and on Day 11 you prove you can read and make one without them.

**What happens off the page.** The studio is a room with tools and a master in it — a mill, a lathe, a printer, a surface plate, a set of gauge blocks, and a machinist who has scrapped parts worth more than a semester's tuition — and the page is the brief. In the room there is shop safety training before anything spins, a weekly bench review of drawings and parts, and the shelf of failures. A learner alone online does the smallest real version: a mechanism designed in free CAD, printed at home or at a library, drawn to standard, measured with a twenty-dollar caliper, and recorded on an inspection sheet; or one part of it ordered from an online machine shop from your drawing alone, which is the truest test there is of whether the drawing speaks. That is a real mechanism. Make it move a hundred times.

## Open questions for Brandon

1. The default mechanisms are drawn from the Workshop's material traditions — truck, fin and box, housing latch, payload quick-release. Should the studio instead fix one mechanism for the whole cohort, so the critiques compare like with like and the shelf of failures accumulates on one design across years?
2. Who is the machinist? The studio needs a master who has run a job shop, not a lab tech. San Diego's boatyards, the race shops in Oceanside and Escondido, and the machine shops serving the Navy are the places to look.
3. Day 12 wants a photograph of corroded hardware on a San Diego pier. Commons may not have one. May a student or faculty photograph be taken for this and released under CC BY-SA, as a standing practice for local images the arcs need?
