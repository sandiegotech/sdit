# Arc — RHET 210 Epistemic Self-Defense

## The course in one paragraph

At the start you take in what you are shown and believe or disbelieve it by feel. At the end you can take any piece of persuasion, a poster, a chart, a speech, a feed, and do three things to it in order: list exactly what it claims, name the techniques it uses on you, and find out the truth of the matter with evidence you can show someone else. The catalog register is logic, statistical reasoning, cognitive bias, and media literacy. The teaching frame is propaganda, information warfare, and the engineered feed: how to know what's true when the environment is adversarial. The course treats this as a martial art. You learn the moves on historical material, where the record is complete and the stakes are cold, so that you can use them on live material, where neither is true. Your information environment is built by people who want things from you. By Day 15 you know how to tell what they want and whether what they said is so.

## The made thing

A forensic teardown of one real piece of persuasion, documented as a dossier. Finished means: the piece itself, reproduced or linked; a claims inventory, every assertion the piece makes or implies, each tagged by kind (fact, value, prediction) and by who benefits if you believe it; a techniques inventory, every move the piece makes on the reader, named with the vocabulary of Days 3, 9, 10, 13 and 14, with the evidence on the page that it is making that move; the truth of the matter, each factual claim checked to a primary source, with the chain of custody written out; a machine test, in which a model was asked to write the piece's case afresh and its output torn down the same way; and a one-page verdict. Eight to twelve pages. It is defended on Day 15 before the seminar, with one peer assigned as the persuader's advocate, whose job is to show that the teardown is itself unfair. The dossier enters the portfolio for the Year 2 gate.

Day 1 introduces it with the first poster. Day 5 scopes it: the learner chooses a case, from the historical list in "The output" or a contemporary piece approved by the master, and writes a one-paragraph statement of who made it and what they wanted. Day 8 begins the claims inventory; Day 10 the techniques inventory; Day 13 the truth of the matter; Day 14 the machine test. By Day 15 the dossier is complete and the learner is answering for it.

## The unassisted examination

Day 11, tools closed, sixty minutes. Three parts. First, a persuasive text the learner has not seen, about 600 words, historical and public domain (a 1918 Liberty Loan appeal, a 1950s civil-defense pamphlet), to be marked by hand: every claim tagged by kind, evidence, and beneficiary; every technique named. Second, one base-rate problem posed in words, no formula given, answered in words with the denominator shown. Third, one short argument to be judged valid or invalid and true or false, separately. Why this, and why Day 11: a model will do all three of these for you, well. That is exactly why the institution must watch you do them alone. The habits have to be reflexive before the last four days hand you the machine as a weapon, and Thucydides, on the page that day, is the oldest statement of the method being examined.

## The output

**What is written each week.** Two streams. The first is the claim log: every week, three claims met in the wild, in a feed, a conversation, an ad, a lecture, each written out in one sentence and tagged three ways: what kind of claim it is, what would count as evidence for it, and who benefits if you believe it. About 150 words a week, due before the day publishes. The second is the dossier, built in stages.

**How it is line-edited.** The claim log comes back with three marks: UNCHECKED (you asserted the claim is false or true without showing how you know), SOURCE? (the claim has no traceable origin on your page), BENEFIT? (you did not say who gains). The dossier drafts are line-edited with the six marks from RHET 110 (CUT, VAGUE, CLAIM?, SO WHAT, PLAINER, LAND), because a teardown that cannot be read is not a defense. One rule is particular to this course: the editor opens every link and reads every source the learner cites. A citation the editor cannot reach is UNCHECKED. Peers edit first, the master second, and the two sets of marks come back together.

**The machine.** Models are open from Day 3 as a fallacy-finder and from Day 8 as a provenance tool, with the standing rule that every finding is checked by the learner and the check written down; the course grades the check, not the finding. On Day 14 the model is used adversarially: asked to write the strongest case for the persuader's position, then torn down. Day 11 is the control.

**The forensic cases.** Historical and public domain, so the record is complete and the page is durable:

- James Montgomery Flagg, *I Want You for U.S. Army* (1917). Four million printed. Who wants what.
- Lewis Carroll's syllogisms, *The Game of Logic* (1886). Valid and absurd.
- Schopenhauer, *The Art of Controversy* (c. 1831): thirty-eight ways to win an argument without being right.
- Hume, "Of Miracles" (1748). Proportioning belief to evidence.
- John Snow's Broad Street map (1854) and his comparison of the two water companies. The denominator.
- Florence Nightingale's *Diagram of the Causes of Mortality in the Army in the East* (1858). A chart built to move Parliament, and true.
- James Lind's scurvy trial (1747, published 1753). The first control.
- Twain, Disraeli, and "lies, damned lies, and statistics" (1907). A quotation with no origin.
- The Müller-Lyer illusion (1889). Knowing does not fix it.
- The Cottingley Fairies (1917–1920) and Arthur Conan Doyle. Wanting to believe.
- Thucydides I.22 (c. 400 BC). The first statement of method.
- Edward Bernays, *Propaganda* (1928), and the Torches of Freedom march (1929). The manufactured event.
- H. R. Hopps, *Destroy This Mad Brute* (1917), and Savile Lumley, *Daddy, what did YOU do in the Great War?* (1915). Fear, shame, the single enemy.
- Philip Graves's exposure in *The Times* (1921) of the *Protocols* as plagiarized from Maurice Joly (1864). The oldest side-by-side teardown; a door.
- Frank Capra, *Why We Fight: Prelude to War* (1942). Mass persuasion made from the enemy's own film.
- *Duck and Cover* (1951). The state reassuring children; a door.
- The OSS *Simple Sabotage Field Manual* (1944). How to wreck an organization from inside with procedure; a door.
- Feynman, Appendix F to the Rogers Commission report (1986). The standard: nature cannot be fooled.

Contemporary cases are doors, and are allowed as the learner's own teardown subject with approval: the Internet Research Agency ads released by the House Intelligence Committee (2018, public records), a current political ad, a viral chart, a product launch. The dossier may quote them under fair use and link to them; the course page does not host them.

## Open questions for Brandon

1. The Protocols/Graves exposé is the canonical forensic teardown and the course names it as a door on Day 13. Is the Institute comfortable naming it on a public page, or should it live only in the master's seminar materials?
2. Should learners be allowed to choose a live political advertisement as their teardown case during an election season, or should contemporary cases be restricted to commercial and cultural persuasion for the public page?
3. Day 14 asks the learner to have a model write propaganda for their case's position and then tear it down. That is the Command of Intelligence move the catalog wants, and it means the course deliberately produces persuasive text that is never published. Is a written rule needed that machine-test outputs stay in the dossier and off the open web?
