HUM 202 — The Industrial Mind

The Canon · 2 units · Semester 4 · Required core — everyone takes it

Overview

The nineteenth century built the factory, the theory of evolution, and the first fears about what we make. It left four ideas running inside every modern maker, and this course reads them in the order they arrived. Shelley: the thing you make may turn and ask you for an account. Darwin: living things were made by no one and change without a plan. Marx: the factory makes the worker as surely as the worker makes the product. Nietzsche: with no maker above you, the remaining task is to make yourself. Shelley’s question — what do you owe the thing you created? — was a ghost story in 1818. It is now an engineering requirement.

None of it is read alone. This is the Double Feature Method: every unit pairs the text + a film + an album, discussed as one conversation. Frankenstein beside Blade Runner and the birth of electronic music; Darwin beside Grizzly Man; Marx beside Metropolis and Kraftwerk; Nietzsche beside Groundhog Day and Hunky Dory. The century that invented the machine and the anxiety about it turns out to be arguing about your work.

One day a week, about ten minutes a sitting, plus the reading that pulls you in. This is the last Canon course of the Foundation, and it ends the week you declare a concentration. It ends the way every SDIT course ends: in a made thing, defended out loud — this time, a thing that can act without you.

The Two Registers

  • Catalog register: Nineteenth-century thought — Marx, Darwin, Nietzsche, Shelley. Close reading, discussion, and critical writing.
  • Teaching frame: Frankenstein + Blade Runner + the birth of electronic music. The century that invented the machine and the anxiety about it. The text, a film, and an album, discussed as one conversation.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course you can:

  1. Read a nineteenth-century primary text closely and accurately reconstruct its central argument and the evidence it rests on. → Breadth of Mind · Critical thinking
  2. Read a modern engineering or design decision as a nineteenth-century argument continued, and say which side you are on and why. → Judgment · Critical thinking
  3. Trace one idea across a text, a film, and an album, and defend the connection with evidence from all three. → Judgment · Critical thinking
  4. Write short critical responses that make a claim, support it from the text, and survive line-editing. → Articulation · Written communication
  5. Make something that acts without you, answer in writing what you owe it, and defend both in seminar. → Workmanship · Articulation · Oral communication

Each outcome maps to the five institutional outcomes (Judgment, Command of Intelligence, Workmanship, Articulation, Breadth of Mind) — the assessment spine of the degree and its accreditation evidence file.

The Made Thing

A made response to one of the century’s four questions — an essay, a short film, a recorded piece, or a designed object — that is, or contains, something you made that can act without you: a patch that plays on its own, a mechanism, a script, a rule set. You then answer Shelley’s question about it in writing. It is defended in seminar at the end of the course, enters your portfolio, and is reviewed at the annual gate, the same week as the Declaration.

How You’re Assessed

  • Short written responses, line-edited and revised — the work is the writing, not a quiz about the reading.
  • The made response, defended out loud in seminar at the end of the course.
  • Periodic unassisted writing. AI tools are open for drafting and study; the institution certifies the human, so part of your writing happens with the tools closed — including a timed response to Marx’s chapter on the working day, and to your own.
  • Everything lands in your portfolio, reviewed against the five outcomes at the end-of-year gate.
