# ART 310 — Interactive Media & Interfaces

**The Depths** · 4 units · Semester 5 · Depth studio — Media & Design · the Crossing for others

## Overview

Games, installations, interfaces — the one art form where the audience acts. A painting is finished when the painter stops; an interactive piece is finished only when someone else picks it up and does something you did not script. The material of this studio is another person's behavior, and the only honest test of the work is a stranger using it while you say nothing.

This is a depth studio, which means a room with a master in it: fifteen people, one working designer with shipped work, one long block a week, and a brief carried from a paragraph on Day 4 to a piece installed in public on Day 12. The page is the brief. Each day gives you one thing to look at — a 1958 oscilloscope tennis game, a B-17 cockpit, a nuclear control room, a Bruegel town square full of children at play — one idea, and one question. The room gives you the stranger, the silence, and the critique. The AI tools stay open for code, assets, and synthetic playtests, and every synthetic claim is checked against a human before it counts.

The course ends the way every SDIT course ends: in a made thing, defended out loud. Here the defense is the observation log — who you watched, what they did, what you changed.

## The Two Registers

- **Catalog register:** Interactive systems design — interfaces, games, installations, human factors.
- **Teaching frame:** The designed experience — what it feels like to use a thing, studied and built.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course you can:

1. Design, prototype, and build an interactive piece that a stranger can use unaided, from paper to installed hardware or software. → *Workmanship*
2. Run a silent observation of real users, record what they did in a fixed form, and change the piece on the evidence. → *Judgment* · *Critical thinking*
3. Read an interface or a space for its affordances, feedback, and human-factors failures, and say what the design should have done. → *Judgment*
4. Use machine intelligence as a collaborator — for code, assets, characters, and synthetic playtests — and state exactly what it made and what stayed yours. → *Command of Intelligence*
5. Scope a brief by subtraction and defend what was cut, with the log as evidence. → *Workmanship · Judgment* · *Oral communication*

*Each outcome maps to the five institutional outcomes (Judgment, Command of Intelligence, Workmanship, Articulation, Breadth of Mind) — the assessment spine of the degree and its accreditation evidence file.*

## The Made Thing

An interactive piece — a small game, an installation, or an interface — with real users: playable, usable, observed. Finished means a stranger uses it for a full session without you speaking; you have watched at least twenty people use it across three sessions in a place you did not control; the piece has changed at least twice because of what you saw; and the observation log exists. It is exhibited at the semester's public exhibition with the log and a one-page account of what changed and why, then enters your portfolio for the Year 3 gate.

## How You're Assessed

- **The brief**, written on Day 4 and revised in writing at every milestone, with the revisions kept.
- **Three critiques:** the silent test of the first playable (Day 8), the on-site critique of the installed piece (Day 12), and the subtractive critique (Day 14) — graded on what you saw and what you did about it, not on polish.
- **The unassisted examination** (Day 10): a sealed brief, paper and cardboard, ninety minutes, one think-aloud test on a classmate, and a spoken account of three changes — no model, no search, no code. The institution certifies the human, so the human is observed reading a user unaided.
- **The exhibition**: the piece installed, the log presented, the questions answered live.
- Everything lands in your **portfolio**, reviewed against the five outcomes at the end-of-year gate.
