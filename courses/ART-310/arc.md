# Arc — ART 310 Interactive Media & Interfaces

## The course in one paragraph

At the start you can build a thing that works when you run it. At the end you can build a thing that works when a stranger picks it up, say why it works, and prove it with a log of the people you watched. The catalog register is interactive systems design — interfaces, games, installations, human factors. The teaching frame is the designed experience: what it feels like to use a thing, studied and built. The gap between those two sentences is the course. Fifteen days carry one brief from a paragraph to a piece installed where people pass. The page does what a page can do: one thing to look at, one idea, one question. The room does what only a room can: the stranger, the master, and the silence while someone fails to find the button.

## The made thing

One interactive piece — a small game, an installation, or an interface — with real users. It is small by design: one place, one kind of person, one thing to do. "Finished" means four things are true. A stranger uses it for a full session without you speaking. You have watched at least twenty people use it across at least three sessions, in a place you did not control. The piece has changed at least twice because of what you saw. The observation log exists, in a fixed form, with dates.

It is exhibited at the semester's public exhibition night, alongside the Workshop teams, with the log and a one-page account of what changed and why. The defense is the log, not the demo. The piece and the log enter the portfolio and are read at the Year 3 gate.

Day 1 introduces the medium. Day 4 scopes it: the brief is written, one paragraph, that day. Day 5 starts it on paper. Day 8 is the first playable in front of a stranger. Day 12 is the installation in public. Day 15 is the exhibition. By Day 4 you know what you are making; by Day 5 you have begun; by Day 15 it is done and watched.

## The unassisted examination

Day 10, in the room, tools closed. A sealed brief is handed out — something like "a device for two strangers to agree on a lunch spot in sixty seconds" — with paper, index cards, cardboard, and ninety minutes. Then one think-aloud test on a classmate who has not seen the brief. Then five spoken minutes on the three changes you would make and why. No model, no search, no code.

Why this: the course's skill is reading a person using a thing, and a model cannot be the person. A paper prototype and a watched test are what a designer does when the power is out, and it is exactly what the AI tools cannot do for you. The Day 14 critique is also live and unaided: you answer the room about your own log.

## The studio

**The brief.** One interactive piece with real users — playable, usable, observed. One place. One kind of person. Unaided use is the pass condition. Written on Day 4, revised in writing at every milestone, and the revisions kept.

**Technique days.** 2 (affordance, human factors), 3 (the feedback loop and response time), 5 (paper prototyping and Wizard-of-Oz testing), 6 (feedback and game feel), 7 (rules as material), 10 (motor targets, Fitts, the body), 11 (installations and the body as controller), 13 (machine intelligence as player, writer, and synthetic tester). Each one is a method the learner applies to their own piece that week.

**Critique days.** 8 (the silent test: a stranger uses the first playable, nobody speaks, the room writes what it saw), 12 (on site: the piece installed in public, the second critique held where it stands), 14 (the subtractive critique: the room names what the piece does not need, and the learner defends the log unaided).

**Milestone days.** 4 (brief written), 8 (first playable), 12 (installed, counting), 15 (exhibited with the log).

**Off the page.** The studio is a room with a master in it, and the page is the brief. The room holds fifteen people and one working designer with shipped work — games, exhibits, or interfaces that real people have used. It meets for one long block each week. In it: the microcontrollers, sensors, projectors, a cabinet, the paper and tape; the host venues the Institute arranges for Day 12 (the Workshop hallway, a café, the Fleet Science Center floor, the New Children's Museum, the Coin-Op Game Room); the observation protocol (silent, timed, a fixed note form, consent on a card); and the AI protocol: models open for code, assets, dialogue, and synthetic playtests, with every synthetic claim checked against a human before it counts. A learner on the open path without the room does all of this with whoever they can find — a roommate, a café, a library table — and the page tells them so.
