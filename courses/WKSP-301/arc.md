# Arc — WKSP 301 The Workshop

## The course in one paragraph

At the start you have declared a concentration and shipped four Foundation builds; by the end you have built, with a team drawn from all three concentrations, a camera rig or housing that gets a client a shot they could not get — in the water, on a board, on a bike, in the air — and you have entered the tradition through your own concentration's materials: the operating end if you are Media, the part a person holds if you are Design, the part that must work on its own if you are Engineering. The catalog register is the fifth course of the eight-semester fabrication and design practicum; the teaching frame is the Workshop in your concentration's materials, with a client, as the rotation continues. The rig is judged by the footage.

## The made thing

A client project, shipped and documented: one camera rig or housing — a water housing for a surf photographer, a board or bike mount for a filmmaker, a pole or crane rig for a documentary crew — delivered to a named client who uses it to get the shot named in the brief. Finished means: it has been in the water or on the mount with a real camera and come back dry and whole; the client has footage from it; the seal and the mount have a written test protocol with results; the rig has a card telling the client how to open it, close it, and check it. Day 1 names the client and the shot; Day 3 writes the brief in the client's terms; Day 5 writes the test protocol before the build; the build runs Weeks 4 to 12. By Day 15 the client has the shot and the log has the footage as its proof.

## The unassisted examination

Day 11's week, tools closed: given a housing or mount you did not build, inspect it, name the three ways it will fail — where water gets in, where it flexes, where it lets go — and say which you would test first and how. Then seal a housing yourself and pass the dry test the examiner chooses. Why: a rig can be bought, and a bought rig can leak. The Workshop certifies that you can look at a thing meant to protect something precious and know where it is weak before the ocean tells you.

## The build and the rotation

**This semester's material.** Camera rigs and housings — the fourth tradition of the rotation and the one every concentration can enter through its own door. Media teams build the operating end: the rig as an instrument for a shot — where the camera goes, how it moves, what it frames. Design teams build the part a person holds: the handle, the latch, the housing's ergonomics, the thing that has to be opened cold and wet with one hand. Engineering teams build the part that must work on its own: the seal, the trigger, the intervalometer, the servo, the firmware. Every team ships one rig; every team is mixed; every rig gets one shot for one client.

**The client.** A photographer or filmmaker with a shot they have given up on — a SIG 201 student, a local surf photographer, a documentary crew, a drone operator who wants a camera where the drone cannot go. The client names the shot on Day 1, approves the brief on Day 3, and takes the shot on Day 12.

**The critique method.** Describe before you judge, with a new rule for the cross-concentration bench: on Day 7 the Engineering member describes the Media decisions, the Media member describes the Design decisions, and so on around the table. The user's knowledge outranks the maker's — Plato said it of flutes; it is truer of housings.

**The exhibition.** None this semester; the deliverable is the shot. The footage and the test log enter the portfolio for the Year 3 gate.

**The documentation discipline.** Three photos and a sentence, continuing. Added: the brief in the client's units — lens, mount, weight, depth, duration (Day 3); the test protocol written before the build (Day 5); the test log with results (Day 9); the drawing from several views (Day 9); and the footage itself, with the shot named, as the final entry (Day 13).

**The rotation across the eight semesters.**

1. **WKSP 101** (Semester 1) — Make anything, and finish it. No assigned tradition; the room's stock pulls most first builds toward the small end of board construction.
2. **WKSP 102** (Semester 2) — Surfboard and skateboard construction. The board goes to the year-end public exhibition.
3. **WKSP 201** (Semester 3) — Amplifier and pedal electronics. The first external client: a musician who will plug it in.
4. **WKSP 202** (Semester 4) — Motorcycle mechanics. A client's machine brought back to running; the last Foundation build and the evidence for the Declaration.
5. **WKSP 301** (Semester 5) — Camera rigs and housings. A client who needs a shot they cannot get; each concentration enters the tradition through its own materials.
6. **WKSP 302** (Semester 6) — Drones and ocean sensors, the sensor end. One instrument that survives a week in saltwater and comes back with data; lighter on purpose, beside the Field Term.
7. **WKSP 401** (Semester 7) — The first-years' build. You lead a WKSP 101 team; the material is whatever they can finish.
8. **WKSP 402** (Semester 8) — Your choice from the five traditions, including the drone end the sixth semester left open. The final build, exhibited beside the capstone.

**What the page is, honestly.** The Workshop is a bench with a drill press, a lathe if you are lucky, O-ring stock, a pressure pot, a bucket, a pier, and someone who has flooded a housing and knows why. The page cannot be that. The fifteen days carry the brief, the test protocol, the technique in order, the critique prompt, and the discipline of the test log. A learner working alone online does the smallest real version: a phone or action camera in a housing they make or modify — a dry bag and a frame, a jar and a mount — tested in a bucket, then in a pool, then in the ocean, for one photographer they know, who gets one shot from it.
