# Arc — SIG 101 How Music Moves

## The course in one paragraph

At the start you hear a song. At the end you hear a room, a microphone, a splice, a mix, a master, a contract, a format, a gatekeeper, a fraction of a cent, and an algorithm — and you can say which of them made the song sound and reach you the way it did. The catalog register is the music industry and its technologies: recording, production, distribution, economics. The teaching frame is the trip a song takes, from the room it was recorded in to the algorithm that surfaced it. You learn the trip by walking it with real recordings, fifteen stops, and then you put a song of your own on the road and watch what happens to it. San Diego supplies the examples: the Casbah, the independent labels that grew out of its stage, the Tijuana transmitter that has been San Diego's radio since 1983, and the ear of anyone who has sat in a car in a parking lot waiting for a song to end.

## The made thing

One of two things, the learner's choice, decided on Day 5.

**A released track.** A recording the learner made or produced, mastered, with complete metadata and rights-clear art, released under their name on Bandcamp on a stated date — and, if they choose, pushed through a distributor to the streaming services. "Finished" means a stranger can press play and the learner can say why every part sounds the way it does.

**A documented production breakdown.** A written and illustrated account of how one existing track was built — room, microphones, takes, edits, arrangement, mix placement, master, format, release path, and how it reached its first listeners — published as a page. "Finished" means a producer who was not in the room could rebuild the decisions, and one person who was in the room (or knows the record well) has confirmed the account is accurate.

Both are defended on Day 15 in five minutes: sixty seconds of the sound, the path it took, the numbers so far. Both enter the portfolio. The thirty-day numbers — plays, completion past thirty seconds, where listeners came from, sales if any; or reads and the confirmation reply for a breakdown — are read at the end-of-year gate.

Day 1 announces it. Day 5 scopes it (track or breakdown; which song; the first listening notes written). Day 7 starts it (the first recording session, or the first full-length listen with the track sheet open). Day 14 gives the breakdown its structure. Day 15 is release day. The weeks between days are when the work happens; the one unit measures the weight of the made thing, not the fifteen sittings.

## The unassisted examination

Week 12, after Day 12, thirty minutes, tools closed, headphones on. The learner hears a recording they have never heard, twice. They write: a decade, with reasons; how it was captured (acoustic horn or microphone, live or multitracked); where three instruments sit in the stereo field; two mix decisions they can point to; and the path the record would have taken to a listener in its own year and in this one, with the money at each step in rough proportion. A model can look up a release date. It cannot hear for you, and this course certifies the ear.

## The signal

The real artifact shipped is the track on Bandcamp (with optional distribution to streaming) or the breakdown page on the Institute's site or the learner's own. The audience is real and small by design: the learner's Bandcamp followers and whoever they tell, the cohort, the Institute's list, and the producer or peer asked to confirm a breakdown. Movement is measured at thirty days: plays, the fraction that played past thirty seconds (the line a stream must cross to count), sources of listeners, downloads or sales; for a breakdown, page reads and the written confirmation that it is accurate. The learner also records the one thing they would change on the next release. Honest small numbers are the standard; a hundred real listens beats a thousand bought ones, and the course says so.

## Rights and sources

- **Day 3, "Whispering" Jack Smith (1925).** Sound recordings fixed in 1925 entered the US public domain on 1 January 2026 under the Music Modernization Act's schedule; confirm the recording date on the Great 78 Project item before publishing, and have an alternative 1925 electrical side ready from the same collection. The underlying composition (Ager/Yellen, 1925) is also public domain in the US.
- **Day 2, Caruso 1907.** Pre-1923 US sound recording; public domain since 2022. Confirm the Commons file is the 1907 Victor take and not a later re-recording.
- **Days 5 and 9, Bandcamp embeds.** Bandcamp embeds are allowed by default but artists can disable them; confirm with Three One G and Swami Records (or ask the labels directly — see Open questions) before embedding. Otherwise link out.
- **Days 3, 4, 7, 10 doors** (Crosby, Les Paul, Iron Butterfly, *American Graffiti*) are in copyright and are doors only, linked to official uploads or to where they can be bought.
- **Day 6, Matt Mayfield's loudness-war animation** is an independent upload; embed status unconfirmed, so it is a door.
- **Day 12, royalty figures.** Per-stream rates change yearly; the day states them as a range and teaches the arithmetic, not the number.
- In-copyright books named as doors (Passman, Azerrad, Witt, Pelly) are quoted at under fifteen words if at all and sent to buy or borrow.
- No Lectio edition is needed for this course.
