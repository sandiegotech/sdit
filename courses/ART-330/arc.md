# Arc — ART 330 Music Production & Sound Design

## The course in one paragraph

At the start you can make a recording. At the end you can make a record: you can place a microphone and say why there, hear a mix as an argument and say what it claims, carve a sound to fit a picture, and release a finished piece to strangers with a production note that another producer could learn from. The catalog register is audio production — recording, synthesis, mixing, sound design. The teaching frame is the studio as instrument: every fader a decision, every mix an argument. The course stands on the Fourier you already own from MATH 120 — a sound is a sum of sines, and here every tool is that sum handled by ear. The page gives one thing to hear and one idea each day. The room gives the monitors, the quiet, and a producer who has heard ten thousand mixes.

## The made thing

A produced and mixed piece — a track of three to six minutes, or a sound-design cue built for a classmate's film or interactive piece — recorded, mixed, mastered, and released on a date set on Day 4. "Finished" means: it plays on the studio monitors, a phone speaker, and a car without surprises; it is released on Bandcamp (or screened with the film it was made for) with credits; the stems, the recall sheet, and a one-page production note exist so another producer could reconstruct the decisions. The exhibition is a listening session: the room plays the finished pieces in order, like a record, for invited strangers, and the learner answers for the choices. The release, the stems, and the note enter the portfolio and are read at the Year 3 gate.

Day 1 introduces the studio as an instrument. Day 2 and 3 are about capture. Day 4 scopes the piece and sets the release date. Day 5 to Day 7 are the technique days of synthesis, EQ, and dynamics, applied to the learner's own material. Day 8 is the first critique on the rough. Day 12 is mix lock. Day 15 is the release. By Day 4 you know what you are making; by Day 8 a rough exists; by Day 15 it is out.

## The unassisted examination

Day 11, in the room, tools closed: no analyzer, no plug-in, no reference. Five short clips — each a before and after — and the learner names the move: an EQ cut and where, compression and roughly how much, a reverb type, a pan, a pitch shift. Then six stems and twenty minutes to set a balance by ear on the monitors, and a hand-written recall sheet of what was done. Then the room listens to the balance and says whether it holds.

Why this: the ear is the instrument the institution certifies. Every tool in the studio will suggest a move; a producer who cannot hear whether the move was right is a passenger. The blind test is what every working engineer does for a living, and it is what no model does for them.

## The studio

**The brief.** One piece, three to six minutes — a track, or a cue for an ART 320 film or ART 310 piece — recorded, mixed, mastered, released on a date set on Day 4, with a reference record it has to sit next to. Written on Day 4; revised in writing at mix lock.

**Technique days.** 2 (capture as a decision), 3 (microphones: pattern, distance, proximity), 5 (synthesis: envelope, source, shape), 6 (EQ and filters as one act — the Fourier made a knob), 7 (dynamics and compression), 9 (stereo and placement), 10 (sound design: making the inaudible audible), 13 (mastering and loudness). Each one is a move the learner makes on their own session that week.

**Critique days.** 8 (three speakers: the rough on the monitors, a phone, and a car; one sentence per listener on what they heard first), 11 (the blind test, then the room judges the balance), 12 (mix lock: the room listens as a listener, start to finish, and says what the piece is about).

**Milestone days.** 4 (brief, reference, release date), 8 (rough exists), 12 (mix locked, stems printed, recall written), 15 (released, with the production note).

**Off the page.** The studio is a room with a master in it, and the page is the brief. The room is a control room and a live room: treated, with monitors that tell the truth, a console or a controller, a modest mic locker, a synthesizer or two, and a producer or mixing engineer with records people have heard. It meets for one long block each week, with booked session time between. The Spreckels Organ Pavilion in Balboa Park, the Museum of Making Music in Carlsbad, and a working San Diego studio are the field visits. The AI tools are open for stem separation, rough arrangement, reference matching, and transcription, and closed on Day 11. A learner on the open path works on headphones and a laptop, mixes in the car, and releases on Bandcamp the same as anyone; the page says so and never pretends the room is not better.
