SIG 301 — How Scenes Form

Culture & Signal · 1 unit · Semester 5 · Required core — everyone takes it

Overview

Every technology and art form you love was invented by a small group in a specific room before it was an industry. Hip-hop was a back-to-school party in a Bronx rec room with a quarter at the door. The modern surfboard came from a Caltech dropout at Windansea who read a Navy manual on planing hulls. The personal computer came out of a garage club with a newsletter. The drum machine under half of modern music was a commercial failure that scenes bought used for a hundred dollars. This course studies those rooms closely — their rules, their passwords, their tools, their media, the bridge people who connect them, and the curve they ride from a few laughed-at adopters to everyone — and it treats them as what they are: research and development labs that nobody funded.

The third Culture & Signal unit is the one most at home in San Diego. The Che Café has run on the same rules since 1980. Chicano Park was built by a scene that occupied the ground under a bridge and would not leave. Tijuana’s Nortec Collective made music for two cities at once. Rodney Mullen, after whom the Institute’s arts wing is named, explains in his own words how a trick becomes open-source the moment it is landed in public.

One day a week, about ten minutes a sitting, and the rest of the week in the field. The course ends in a made thing: a field study of a living scene, written from inside after weeks with a notebook, and handed back to the room before it is defended in seminar.

The Two Registers

  • Catalog register: Sociology of cultural movements — subcultures, scenes, and innovation diffusion.
  • Teaching frame: Subcultures as R&D labs: punk, hip-hop, skate, surf, internet scenes — how small groups invent what everyone uses later.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course you can:

  1. Walk into a scene and name its rules, its password, its medium, its tools, and its bridge people, from observation rather than from what it says about itself. → Judgment · Critical thinking
  2. Place a scene on the diffusion curve and say what stage it is in — lab, named, incorporated — with evidence. → Judgment · Breadth of Mind · Critical thinking
  3. Trace how five scenes — punk, hip-hop, skate, surf, and the early personal computer — invented what became industries, and say what they had in common. → Breadth of Mind
  4. Conduct participant observation honestly: keep separate notebooks, ask permission, record what nobody writes down, and state what you cannot know from where you stand. → Judgment · Critical thinking
  5. Write a field study that the scene it describes recognizes, argues with, and could not have written itself. → Breadth of Mind · Written communication

Each outcome maps to the five institutional outcomes (Judgment, Command of Intelligence, Workmanship, Articulation, Breadth of Mind) — the assessment spine of the degree and its accreditation evidence file.

The Made Thing

A field study of a living scene you belong to or can enter honestly — 2,500 to 4,000 words with photographs or recordings, written from inside after at least eight weeks of being there with a notebook. It names the room and its rules, who is in and who is out, what counts as good work, how a newcomer learns, the tools and the medium, the bridge people, and where the scene sits on the curve — and it says plainly what you cannot know from where you stand. It is finished when at least two named members of the scene have read it and their responses are recorded in it. It is defended twice: to the scene first, then in seminar. It enters your portfolio and is read at the annual gate.

How You’re Assessed

  • Weekly field notes, kept in three notebooks — what happened, what they said, what you think — and reviewed for the separation.
  • The field study, handed back to the scene and then defended in seminar.
  • An unassisted examination in the thirteenth week: notebooks closed, forty minutes, your scene’s unwritten constitution from memory, and a paragraph on what you cannot know. AI tools are open for everything else; the institution certifies the human, and fieldwork knowledge lives in the person who did the fieldwork.
  • Everything lands in your portfolio, reviewed against the five outcomes at the end-of-year gate.
