# Arc — SIG 301 How Scenes Form

## The course in one paragraph

At the start you are in a scene or near one and cannot see it. At the end you can walk into any room where people are making something together — a skate spot, a label, a Discord, a mural crew, a surf break with a club — and name its rules, its password, its medium, its bridge people, its tools, and which stage of the curve it is on, and you can write that account from inside without betraying the room. The catalog register is the sociology of cultural movements: subcultures, scenes, and innovation diffusion. The teaching frame is subcultures as R&D labs — punk, hip-hop, skate, surf, internet scenes — how small groups invent what everyone uses later. The material is San Diego's: Windansea and the Navy book that made the modern surfboard, the Che Café, Chicano Park, the border, the Casbah, and Rodney Mullen, after whom the Institute's arts wing is named. The made thing is a field study of a living scene, documented from inside and handed back to the room.

## The made thing

A field study of a living scene the learner belongs to or can enter honestly — 2,500 to 4,000 words with photographs or recordings, written from inside after at least eight weeks of being there with a notebook. It names the room and its rules, who is in and who is out, what counts as good work, how a newcomer learns, the tools the scene runs on, its medium, its bridge people, and where it sits on the diffusion curve — and it says plainly what the writer cannot know from where they stand.

"Finished" means the study has been read by at least two named members of the scene and their responses — corrections, objections, agreement — are recorded in it. It is defended twice: in seminar on Day 15, and to the scene, which gets it first. It is published on the Institute's site, or as a zine for the room if the scene would rather not be online; the learner decides with the scene.

Day 1 announces it. Day 5 scopes it: which scene, by name, and the learner's honest position in it (member, regular, newcomer). Day 7 starts it: the notebook opens, the first field day is written up. Day 13 teaches the method for the write-up. Day 14 asks which stage the scene is in. Day 15 is the hand-back. The study enters the portfolio and is read at the end-of-year gate.

## The unassisted examination

Week 13, after Day 13, forty minutes, notebooks and tools closed. The learner writes their scene's unwritten constitution from memory: who is in and who is out; what counts as good work and who decides; how a newcomer learns; the tools and the medium; the bridge people; the stage on the curve — then one paragraph on what they cannot know from where they stand. Fieldwork knowledge lives in the person who did the fieldwork. A model can summarize a scene it has read about; it cannot have been in the room.

## The signal

The artifact is a field study, so the signal is the study handed back to the scene it describes. The audience is the scene itself, by name, and then the cohort and whoever reads the Institute's site. Movement is measured in a way fitted to the thing: how many named members read it; what they said, recorded verbatim with permission; whether anyone in the scene passed it to someone else; page reads or copies handed out. The numbers here are small by nature and the course says so. The test is not reach but recognition: did the people in the room see themselves, and did they argue?

## Open questions for Brandon

1. **Rodney Mullen himself.** Day 3 is built on his talk and the wing carries his name. Is the relationship such that he would record the "ask someone" for that day, or sit for a Perspectives lesson the course could embed and own?
2. **Which scenes are allowed.** Should the field study be restricted to a scene the learner can be physically present in (San Diego and Tijuana), or may an online scene — a Discord, an open-source project, a forum — count? Day 10 argues it can; the Malinowski method on Day 13 assumes a veranda.
3. **The Che Café and Chicano Park as partners.** Both are living institutions with their own politics about being studied. Should the Institute approach them formally before learners start showing up with notebooks, and who makes that approach?
