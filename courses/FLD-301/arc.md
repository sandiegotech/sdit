# Arc — FLD 301 Field Term

## The course in one paragraph

At the start you have three years of SDIT work behind you and none of it done for anyone but the Institute. At the end you have spent a term inside a working organization — an engineering firm, a lab, a studio, a label, a venture — under a named mentor, doing work that someone else needed done, and you can give a plain written account of what that work was and what it taught you. The catalog register is a supervised field placement in a partner organization, with faculty advising. The teaching frame is one full term embedded in a real organization under a designated mentor, doing real work. This is not a subject course. There is nothing to learn here that the page can teach you; the page prepares you, keeps you honest once a week, and makes you write it down at the end. The fifteen days are the preparation (Days 1 to 4), eight weekly field-journal prompts carried into the placement (Days 5 to 12), the mentor evaluation (Day 13), and the written field report (Days 14 and 15). Semester 6 carries a lighter load on purpose; this is why.

## The made thing

Three things, assessed together: the **mentor evaluation**, the **portfolio of field work**, and the **written field report**.

- The mentor evaluation is a one-page form on the five outcomes, filled out by the mentor at the end of the term, read with you in a conversation you asked for. You bring the form (Day 13). Finished means: filled out, signed, discussed, and in your portfolio next to the work it describes.
- The portfolio of field work is what you actually made or did, in whatever form the organization allowed you to keep: drawings, code, cuts, mixes, reports, a build, a fixture, a process you documented. Where confidentiality forbids the thing itself, the portfolio holds a description the mentor has approved. Finished means: the mentor could look at it and say "yes, that is what they did."
- The written field report is 2,500 to 4,000 words: what the organization is and how it works; what you were asked to do and what you did; what the work taught you that the Institute could not; what you would do differently; what you want next. It is written for the faculty adviser and for the first-year who will read it in three years. One section is written unaided (see below). Finished means: delivered to the adviser and the mentor on the same day, and defended in a half-hour conversation with the adviser at the term's end.

Day 1 introduces all three. Day 4 scopes them in the placement agreement, which names what you will make and what the mentor will evaluate. Day 5 starts the journal that becomes the report's raw material. Day 11 starts the portfolio in earnest. Day 14 drafts the report. By Day 15 it is delivered.

## The unassisted examination

The field report's third section — "what the work taught me that the Institute could not" — is written in one sitting with the tools closed, in Week 14, roughly 800 words, and the adviser reads it beside the machine-assisted sections. The reason is plain: a field report drafted by a model is an account of work the model did not witness. The institution certifies that you were in the room. The mentor conversation on Day 13 and the report defense at term's end are also unaided by nature.

## The placement

**Preparation (Days 1 to 4).** In Semester 5 the adviser and the student shortlist three organizations; by the first day of Semester 6 one has said yes. The Institute keeps a list of partner organizations with named mentors who have agreed to the terms below; the student may also bring their own. The standard is a working organization that ships something, a mentor with a body of work who will spend one hour a week with you, and real responsibilities, meaning work someone would have had to do if you were not there. Days 1 to 3 are about walking in well: what you look like to the people inside, what an apprentice adds, who has to say yes and what their objections are. Day 4 is the agreement.

**The agreement (Day 4).** One page, signed by student, mentor, and adviser before the first week in the field. It names: the mentor; the hours (the Institute's standard is full-time for the term; the self-arranged version below may be less, stated); the responsibilities, as specifically as the organization can say in advance; what the student will make and keep for the portfolio; what is confidential and what is not; the weekly hour with the mentor; the two check-ins with the adviser (Week 5 and Week 10); the evaluation form the mentor will fill out and when; and the date the report is due. An agreement is what makes this a placement rather than a job or a favor. Franklin signed indentures at twelve that bound both sides; the point is that both sides are bound.

**The weekly field journal (Days 5 to 12).** Eight prompts, one a week, each answered in under a page, written the same week, kept in one place. They are the report's raw material and the adviser's window into the placement. The prompts, in order:

| Week | Prompt |
|---|---|
| 1 | Who knows what. Map the room: every person you have met, what they know that you do not, and one question you will ask each of them. |
| 2 | What contradicted you. One thing you assumed about this work or this place that turned out wrong, written down the day it happened. |
| 3 | What you finished. The first task you completed to the organization's standard, and who checked it. |
| 4 | Your erratum. One mistake you made, how it was found, how it was corrected, and what it cost. |
| 5 | What the mentor sees. The midterm conversation: what the mentor says you do well, what they say you do not, in their words. |
| 6 | The unwritten rules. Three customs of the place no one explained to you, and what each one is for. |
| 7 | Ten things you can do. A list, in plain claims, of what you have actually done this term — the portfolio begins here. |
| 8 | Retrospect. What you would tell yourself on the first morning, and what the term changed. |

**The mentor evaluation (Day 13).** A one-page form on the five outcomes — Judgment, Command of Intelligence, Workmanship, Articulation, Breadth of Mind — each with a four-point scale and two lines of comment, plus one open question: "Would you have this person back, and in what role?" The student brings the form in Week 13, the mentor fills it out, and they read it together in the weekly hour. The form goes to the adviser and into the portfolio. It is read beside the work, never alone.

**The report (Days 14 and 15).** Drafted in Week 14 from the journal and the portfolio, with the unaided section written in one sitting. Delivered in Week 15 to the adviser and the mentor, then defended with the adviser. Graded by the adviser on the five outcomes with the mentor's form beside it.

**The learner alone online.** You cannot be placed by an institution that has not yet enrolled you. Run the Field Term as a self-arranged placement, and say so on the agreement. What counts: a job you already hold, if you can name a mentor there and write the agreement with them; a volunteer role with real responsibilities; a shop, studio, lab, or small company that will take you a day or two a week for fifteen weeks; a venture you join for a term. What does not count: watching, shadowing, or a placement without a named mentor who will give you one hour a week. The hours may be fewer than full-time; write the real number on the agreement. Your faculty adviser is whoever you can find who has done this kind of work and will read eight journal entries and one report — a former teacher, a working professional, a member of a local meetup. The mentor form is the same form. The report is the same report. The standard is the same: real work, witnessed, written down.
