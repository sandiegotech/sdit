# Arc — ENGR 410 Robotics & Autonomous Systems

## The course in one paragraph

At the start you can make a machine move under command, read a sensor, and train a model. At the end you can make a machine that decides for itself — perceives the world, knows where it is, plans a path, controls its body, handles the thing that was not there at rehearsal, and stops safely when it should — and you can demonstrate it live, in one take, in a parking lot, with witnesses. The catalog register is robotics — perception, control, planning, autonomy. The teaching frame is perception, control, and autonomy in the physical world, where the demo meets the parking lot. Everything you have made converges here: the mechanism from ENGR 310, the instrument from ENGR 320, the system from CS 310. The machine has to work in the lot, not on the slide.

## The made thing

An autonomous system demonstrated live, in the world, doing its job. Concretely: a ground robot (the default) or a small unmanned surface vessel, built or adapted by a team of two or three, that is given a destination or a task in a real, uncontrolled space — the Institute's parking lot, a stretch of Mission Bay — and completes it without a human touching the controls, while handling at least one obstacle placed after the last rehearsal by someone outside the team. The job must be real and stated in a sentence: "Survey the lot, find the marked space, report whether it is occupied, and return." "Hold station at this buoy for ten minutes in the afternoon wind, carrying the ENGR 320 instrument." "Finished" means: the system has done the job ten times in rehearsal with a recorded success rate; it has an emergency stop, a geofence, and a written safety case; its logs from every run are kept; and on Day 15 it does the job live, one take, in front of the room, with a range safety officer's hand on the stop. The demonstration is followed by the logs and the account: what it saw, what it decided, where it was wrong.

Day 1 states the premise. Day 2 is the brief: team, platform, job, and the sentence. Day 5 is the platform moving under command. Day 10 is a full autonomous rehearsal in the venue. Day 12 is the safety case. Day 14 is the final critique. Day 15 is the live demonstration.

## The unassisted examination

Day 11, tools closed, the week after the first full rehearsal. You are given a step-response plot from a controller and asked what is wrong with it and what you would change; a grid map with a start, a goal, and obstacles, and asked to plan a path by hand and say why it is the one a planner would find; and a page of odometry readings with one landmark fix, and asked where the robot is and how sure you are. Ninety minutes, pencil. Why this: Armstrong flew the last few hundred feet to the Moon by hand because the autopilot was taking him into a boulder field. The autonomy you build will hand you the problem at the worst moment, and the institution certifies the human who can take it.

## The studio

**The brief.** Build a machine that does one real job in a real place without you, and prove it ten times before you prove it once in public. Default platform: a wheeled rover the size of a cooler, with a camera, a depth or lidar sensor, wheel encoders, an inertial unit, and a compute board that can run a small model. Alternative: an unmanned surface vessel for Mission Bay, which trades the parking lot's cones for wind and current. The job is chosen on Day 2 and written in one sentence that a stranger could judge pass or fail.

**Technique days.** Day 2 (the loop in a world that does not wait), Day 3 (localization), Day 4 (perception as guessing), Day 6 (control as a conversation with error), Day 7 (planning and replanning), Day 8 (platforms — wheels, legs, hulls, rotors), Day 9 (learning in the loop, and when not to), Day 12 (safety as the first feature), Day 13 (the job, not the demo).

**Critique days.** Day 5 (it moves — and why it did not go straight), Day 10 (rehearsal in the venue, logs and video reviewed), Day 14 (the fall critique: every failure from ten rehearsals, and the fix for each). The critique rule: the video and the log play together, so the room sees what the robot saw when it did what it did.

**Milestone days.** Day 2 (brief), Day 5 (moves under command, stops on command), Day 10 (full autonomous run in the venue), Day 12 (safety case signed), Day 15 (live, one take).

**How the AI tools are used, and then closed.** Open for everything: writing drivers and planners, training the perception model, generating test scenarios, reading logs. Learned components are allowed where the harness from CS 310 shows they beat the hand-coded version, and forbidden in the safety chain — the e-stop, the geofence, and the speed limit are plain code you can read aloud. On Day 11 you tune, plan, and localize by hand.

**What happens off the page.** The studio is a room with tools and a master in it — a parking lot you can close, a stretch of bay you can use, a range safety officer, a fleet of platforms that have been crashed before, and someone who has fielded unmanned systems for a living and watched one fail in front of a customer — and the page is the brief. In the room there is a safety process no run skips, a rehearsal schedule, and a wall of logs. A learner alone online does the smallest real version: a small wheeled robot kit with a camera, a hallway or a driveway, a job stated in one sentence, ten rehearsals logged, and one obstacle placed by someone else, filmed in a single take and posted with the log. That is autonomy in the world, at the scale of one. The hallway does not know it is small.

| 4 | Seeing Is Guessing | NASA/JPL navigation-camera image from the Perseverance rover, Mars · Wikimedia Commons, NASA · public domain (confirm file) | The camera gives pixels; the lidar gives distances; neither gives the world. | 5 | Drive in a Straight Line | Leonardo da Vinci's drawing of a self-propelled cart, Codex Atlanticus, f. 812r, about 1478 · Wikimedia Commons · public domain (confirm file) | First critique. Today your platform moves under command and stops on command, and the room asks why it curved. Unequal wheels, slip, a motor that lags — a straight line is already a control problem. | 6 | Control Is a Conversation With Error | Photograph of Lawrence Sperry's 1914 demonstration of the gyroscopic autopilot — hands off the controls, mechanic standing on the wing · Wikimedia Commons · public domain (confirm file) | Sperry stood with his hands up while the airplane flew itself over the Seine. | 7 | Plan the Path, Expect the Cone | Animation of the A* search algorithm finding a path through obstacles · Wikimedia Commons, File:Astar_progress_animation.gif · CC BY-SA 3.0 | A map, a cost for every cell, a start and a goal, and a search that expands the cheapest promising step first — that is path planning, and it finds the shortest way through the lot you mapped yesterday. ## Open questions for Brandon

1. The parking lot is the Institute's; the bay is the Port's. Should the arc commit to the ground rover as the default for the first cohort and hold the unmanned surface vessel for a second year, so the safety process is proven on asphalt before water?
2. Who is the range safety officer and the master? The brief wants someone who has fielded unmanned systems — the Navy's unmanned programs at Point Loma, the drone firms in Kearny Mesa and Carlsbad, or a retired test-range officer. The DEUS posture of the course depends on this person being real.
3. Should the ENGR 410 demonstration be public — the Institute's own small Grand Challenge, open to the neighborhood, the way the Workshop exhibitions are — or kept to the room and the examiners until the safety process has a year behind it?
