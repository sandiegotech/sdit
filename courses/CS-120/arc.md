# Arc — CS 120 Computational Thinking & Systems

## The course in one paragraph

At the start, the machine you program is a box that does what you type. At the end, there is no box: you have built a working computer upward from a single kind of gate, written a program for it in its own machine language, and watched your program run on hardware you understand at every layer. The catalog register is computer organization and systems — logic gates, memory, processors, operating systems, networks. The teaching frame is How Machines Think: how machines actually work, from logic gates up, every layer explained by building it. You leave able to say, for any device you touch, what happens between your finger and the result — and to say where in that stack the assistant you talk to actually lives.

## The made thing

A working computer built upward from gates — simulated or breadboarded — that runs a program you wrote. The default path is the Hack computer from the Nand2Tetris project, built chip by chip in the free, browser-based Nand2Tetris web IDE (nand2tetris.github.io/web-ide; MIT-licensed software, no account): from Nand to Not, And, Or, Xor, Mux; to adders and the ALU; to the flip-flop, register, RAM, and program counter; to the CPU and the whole machine. The hardware door, open every day for those who want it, follows Ben Eater's breadboard series: the same ideas in transistors and 74-series chips, at the cost of parts and evenings. "Finished" means the simulated computer passes every chip's test script, runs a machine-language program you wrote by hand (the default: a program that reads a number from memory and writes its running total, or draws a line on the screen map), and ships with a one-page account of each layer in your own words. It is exhibited by running it in front of a classmate, who picks a chip and asks you to explain it from the ones below.

Day 1 names the thing. Day 2 opens the simulator and the first HDL file. Day 5 has the ALU passing its tests. Day 10 has memory and a program written. Day 11 wires the CPU. Day 15 runs your program on your computer. The learner knows what they are making from Day 1 and has begun by Day 2.

## The unassisted examination

Twice, tools closed, on paper. **Day 8:** draw a full adder from gates, add two four-bit numbers by hand in two's complement, and explain in three sentences why a latch remembers. **Day 14:** given five lines of Hack assembly, translate them to binary by hand and trace what each does to the registers and memory; then explain, in your own words, what the operating system did between you double-clicking a file and the file opening. These are examined because they are the two hinges of the stack — arithmetic from switches, and instructions as numbers — and a learner who has only watched the assistant write HDL has not built anything. Pass or revise; repeated at the annual gate.

## The build sequence

**Day 5:** every elementary gate (Not, And, Or, Xor, Mux, DMux, and their 16-bit forms), the half and full adder, the 16-bit adder, and the Hack ALU — all written in HDL in the web IDE and passing their test scripts. On the hardware door: a NAND gate from two transistors, and a one-bit adder from chips, on a breadboard.

**Day 10:** the sequential chips — Bit, Register, RAM8 through RAM16K, the program counter — passing their tests; the fetch-execute cycle understood as a clock tick; and your first program written in Hack assembly, assembled, and running on the web IDE's built-in CPU emulator. On the hardware door: a 555 clock blinking and a register latching a value.

**Day 15:** the CPU and Computer chips built from your own parts, your program running on them, the account written, the demonstration given.

**How the AI tools are used:** open from Day 2. The assistant may draft HDL, explain a failing test line, or propose a program. The rule of the course is that the test script, not the assistant, says whether a chip works — and every day the assistant drafts something, you must make it fail on a case you chose before you trust it. Days 3, 6, and 11 ask you to find the assistant's wrong chip; Day 12 asks what the assistant is, in terms of the layers you built. **How the human is examined unaided:** Days 8 and 14, as above, plus the standing rule: any chip in your computer may be explained from the chips below it, on request.
